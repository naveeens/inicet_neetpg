# Neuro Ophthalmology

## Pupillary Pathways & Abnormalities

### Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect (RAPD / Marcus Gunn Pupil)
- Detected by **swinging flashlight test**
- Indicates unilateral/asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal disease (afferent pathway defect)
- On swinging light to affected eye → paradoxical **dilation** instead of constriction (both pupils constrict less when light shines in the diseased eye vs. the normal eye)
- Causes: optic neuritis, optic nerve compression, advanced glaucoma, CRAO, severe retinal detachment — NOT caused by a dense cataract or refractive error alone

### Argyll Robertson Pupil
- Classic in **neurosyphilis** (also diabetes)
- **"Accommodates but does not react"** — light-near dissociation: pupil fails to constrict to light but constricts normally on accommodation/convergence
- Small, irregular, bilateral pupils

### Adie's Tonic Pupil
- Due to ciliary ganglion damage (often idiopathic/post-viral)
- Dilated pupil, poor/absent light reaction, slow tonic constriction to accommodation (near response present but sluggish and slow to redilate)
- Denervation supersensitivity to dilute (0.1%) pilocarpine — constricts (unlike normal pupil)
- Often part of **Holmes-Adie syndrome** (with absent deep tendon reflexes)

### Horner's Syndrome
- Sympathetic pathway lesion → triad: **Ptosis (mild, due to Müller's muscle), Miosis, Anhidrosis** (± enophthalmos apparent)
- Pharmacological localization:
  - **Cocaine test**: fails to dilate affected pupil (confirms Horner's — cocaine blocks NE reuptake, needs intact sympathetic tone)
  - **Hydroxyamphetamine (paredrine) test**: dilates if lesion is preganglionic (1st/2nd order neuron); fails to dilate if postganglionic (3rd order neuron) — releases stored NE from intact terminals
  - **Apraclonidine test**: reverses anisocoria (denervation supersensitivity) — modern alternative to cocaine test
- Causes: Pancoast tumor (apex of lung — classic), carotid dissection (painful Horner's — emergency), brainstem stroke (lateral medullary/Wallenberg syndrome), neck trauma/surgery

## Cranial Nerve Palsies (Ocular Motility)

### Third Nerve (Oculomotor) Palsy
- Presents with: **ptosis** (complete, levator palpebrae), eye deviated **"down and out"** (unopposed lateral rectus/CN VI and superior oblique/CN IV action), **mydriasis** (if pupil involved)
- **"Rule of the pupil"**:
  - **Pupil involved (dilated, non-reactive)** → suspect **compressive** cause (e.g., **posterior communicating artery aneurysm**) — surgical emergency, urgent imaging (CT/MR angiography)
  - **Pupil spared** → suspect **ischemic/microvascular** cause (diabetes, hypertension — vasa nervorum infarction of central fibers, sparing peripherally located pupillary fibers) — manage medically, usually resolves in weeks

### Fourth Nerve (Trochlear) Palsy — Superior Oblique
- Vertical diplopia, worse on **looking down and toward the nose** (e.g., reading, going downstairs)
- Head tilt to the **opposite/contralateral shoulder** (compensatory) to minimize diplopia
- **Bielschowsky head tilt test**: hypertropia worsens on tilting head to the same side as the affected eye
- Most common cause: congenital (decompensating); trauma is most common acquired cause

### Sixth Nerve (Abducens) Palsy — Lateral Rectus
- Convergent squint (esotropia), failure of abduction, horizontal diplopia worse at distance
- Long intracranial course → most vulnerable to **raised ICP** (false localizing sign) and skull base pathology
- Causes: microvascular (DM/HTN), raised ICP, trauma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma (Gradenigo syndrome), pontine lesions

## Optic Nerve Disorders

### Papilledema
- **Bilateral disc swelling due to raised intracranial pressure** (must distinguish from unilateral disc swelling of optic neuritis/papillitis)
- Vision typically preserved early (unlike optic neuritis); enlarged blind spot; may later develop constricted fields
- Causes: idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH/pseudotumor cerebri — classically obese young women), space-occupying lesion, venous sinus thrombosis, malignant hypertension
- Associated symptoms: headache (worse lying down/morning), nausea/vomiting, transient visual obscurations

### Optic Neuritis
- Inflammatory demyelination of optic nerve; strongly associated with **multiple sclerosis** (may be first presentation)
- Typically **unilateral**, acute/subacute vision loss, **pain on eye movement** (key differentiator from papilledema), central scotoma, dyschromatopsia (red-green desaturation)
- RAPD present; disc may be normal (retrobulbar neuritis) or swollen (papillitis)
- MRI brain/orbits: demyelinating plaques (predicts MS conversion risk)
- Treatment: IV methylprednisolone (hastens recovery, does not change final visual outcome); oral steroids alone contraindicated (↑ recurrence risk per Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial)

### Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (AION)
| Feature | Arteritic (AAION) | Non-arteritic (NAION) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | **Giant cell (temporal) arteritis** | Microvascular (HTN, DM, atherosclerosis) |
| Age | Elderly (>70) | 50s–60s |
| Associated symptoms | Jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, temporal artery tenderness, headache, PMR | None systemic |
| Labs | ESR/CRP markedly ↑ | Normal |
| Urgency | Emergency — **high-dose systemic steroids immediately** to prevent fellow eye involvement/blindness; temporal artery biopsy to confirm | Supportive; no proven treatment |
| Disc appearance | Chalky white disc edema | Pale/hyperemic disc edema, "disc at risk" (small crowded disc) in fellow eye |

## Visual Field Defects & Lesion Localization
| Lesion Site | Field Defect |
|---|---|
| Optic nerve | Central scotoma / monocular vision loss |
| **Optic chiasm** | **Bitemporal hemianopia** (classic: pituitary adenoma compressing chiasm from below/craniopharyngioma from above) |
| Optic tract | Contralateral homonymous hemianopia (incongruous) |
| **Optic radiation — temporal lobe (Meyer's loop)** | Contralateral **homonymous superior quadrantanopia** ("pie in the sky") |
| **Optic radiation — parietal lobe** | Contralateral homonymous **inferior** quadrantanopia |
| Occipital cortex (primary visual cortex) | Contralateral homonymous hemianopia, **highly congruous**, often with **macular sparing** (dual blood supply — PCA + MCA collateral) |

- Rule of thumb: the further back the lesion (toward occipital cortex), the more congruous the field defect

## High-Yield One-Liners
- RAPD (Marcus Gunn pupil) indicates optic nerve or severe retinal pathology, never isolated cataract or refractive error
- Argyll Robertson pupil: "accommodates but doesn't react" — think neurosyphilis
- Adie's tonic pupil shows denervation supersensitivity to dilute pilocarpine
- Third nerve palsy with a dilated, non-reactive pupil = aneurysm until proven otherwise (surgical emergency); pupil-sparing = likely microvascular/ischemic
- Fourth nerve palsy: vertical diplopia worse looking down/in; compensatory head tilt away from lesion
- Sixth nerve palsy is the most common false localizing sign of raised ICP
- Papilledema = bilateral, vision preserved early, no pain; Optic neuritis = usually unilateral, painful eye movement, early vision loss
- AAION (GCA) is a same-day steroid emergency to save the fellow eye — check ESR/CRP in any elderly patient with sudden vision loss
- Bitemporal hemianopia = chiasmal lesion, classically pituitary adenoma
- Occipital lobe lesions produce highly congruous homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing
