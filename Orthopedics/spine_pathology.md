# Spine Pathology

## Cervical Spondylosis
- Degenerative disc disease + facet joint arthritis of cervical spine, common in middle-aged/elderly
- Clinical: neck pain/stiffness, radiculopathy (if nerve root compression), myelopathy (if cord compression — spastic gait, hyperreflexia, Lhermitte's sign)
- Imaging: MRI is investigation of choice for cord/root compression
- Management: conservative (cervical collar, physiotherapy, NSAIDs) for radiculopathy; surgery (anterior cervical discectomy/laminectomy) for progressive myelopathy or refractory radiculopathy

## Prolapsed Intervertebral Disc (PIVD) — Lumbar
- Most common levels: **L4-L5 and L5-S1** (highest mechanical stress)
- Nerve root affected is typically the one exiting below the level of herniation (e.g., L4-L5 disc → L5 root; posterolateral herniation compresses the traversing/lower root)
| Level | Root affected | Motor weakness | Sensory loss | Reflex lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L3-L4 | L4 | Knee extension (quadriceps) | Medial leg | Knee jerk |
| L4-L5 | L5 | Ankle/great toe dorsiflexion (foot drop) | Dorsum of foot/1st web space | None reliable (no specific reflex) |
| L5-S1 | S1 | Ankle plantarflexion, foot eversion | Lateral foot/sole | Ankle jerk |

- Clinical tests: **Straight Leg Raise (SLR)** — positive if pain reproduced at <60–70° (Lasègue's sign), suggests L4-S1 root tension; crossed SLR is more specific though less sensitive; Femoral stretch test for upper lumbar (L2-L4) roots
- Red flag — **Cauda equina syndrome**: bilateral leg pain/weakness, saddle anesthesia, urinary retention/incontinence, decreased anal tone → surgical EMERGENCY, needs emergent MRI + decompression within 24–48 hours
- Management: most resolve with conservative treatment (rest briefly, physiotherapy, NSAIDs, epidural steroid injections); surgery (discectomy) for cauda equina syndrome, progressive neurological deficit, or failed conservative treatment (typically after 6 weeks)

## Spondylolisthesis
- Anterior slippage of one vertebra over the one below, most common at L5-S1
- Types: isthmic (spondylolysis — pars interarticularis defect, most common in young athletes, e.g., fast bowlers/gymnasts), degenerative (older adults, facet arthropathy), traumatic, congenital, pathological
- **Meyerding grading** (based on % of anterior slip of vertebral body):
  - Grade I: 0–25%
  - Grade II: 25–50%
  - Grade III: 50–75%
  - Grade IV: 75–100%
  - Grade V (spondyloptosis): >100% (complete slip off)
- "Scotty dog" sign on oblique X-ray — collar defect represents spondylolysis (pars defect)
- Management: conservative (bracing, activity restriction, core strengthening) for low-grade; surgical fusion for high-grade/progressive slip or neurological symptoms

## Scoliosis
- Lateral curvature of spine with rotation, measured by **Cobb angle** (angle between perpendiculars to end vertebrae most tilted from horizontal)
- Idiopathic scoliosis (most common type) — typically presents in adolescent females (Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis, AIS)
- **Risser sign**: grading of iliac crest apophysis ossification (0–5), used to assess skeletal maturity/remaining growth potential — important for treatment decisions and risk of curve progression
- Management:
  - Cobb angle <25°: observation
  - 25–40–45° (skeletally immature, growth remaining): bracing
  - >40–45° or progressive despite bracing: surgical correction (spinal fusion with instrumentation)

## Kyphosis
- **Scheuermann's disease**: juvenile kyphosis, wedging of ≥3 consecutive vertebrae (>5° each) on X-ray, thoracic spine, adolescents; management conservative (bracing) unless severe (>75°, then surgery)
- Postural kyphosis — corrects with active extension (unlike structural causes)

## Spinal Tuberculosis (Pott's Disease)
- Most common site of skeletal TB; most common site within spine = **lower thoracic and thoracolumbar** vertebrae
- Pathology: starts in anterior vertebral body (paradiscal most common) → spreads across disc space to adjacent vertebra (disc relatively resistant to TB, unlike pyogenic infection) → vertebral body destruction → anterior wedging → **kyphotic deformity (gibbus)**
- Cold abscess formation — can track along fascial planes (psoas abscess presenting in groin, common)
- Clinical: constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats), back pain, gibbus deformity, neurological deficit if cord compression (Pott's paraplegia)
- Investigations: MRI is best for early diagnosis and extent of cord compression; X-ray shows disc space narrowing + vertebral body destruction late
- Management: ATT for 12–18 months is mainstay; surgery (decompression + stabilization) indicated for neurological deficit, significant instability, or large abscess not responding to medical treatment

## Spinal Trauma
- **Denis three-column classification** (thoracolumbar spine): Anterior column (anterior 2/3 of vertebral body + disc), Middle column (posterior 1/3 of vertebral body + posterior longitudinal ligament), Posterior column (posterior elements — pedicles, facets, lamina, ligamentum flavum, spinous process); ≥2 column involvement = unstable
- Fracture patterns: compression (anterior column only, stable), burst (anterior + middle column, retropulsed fragment into canal), flexion-distraction (chance fracture — seat-belt injury), fracture-dislocation (most unstable, all 3 columns + translation)
- **ASIA (American Spinal Injury Association) impairment scale**:
  - A: Complete — no motor/sensory function in sacral segments S4-S5
  - B: Sensory incomplete — sensory but no motor function preserved below level
  - C: Motor incomplete — motor function preserved, more than half of key muscles below level <grade 3
  - D: Motor incomplete — motor function preserved, at least half of key muscles below level ≥grade 3
  - E: Normal
- **Spinal shock** vs **Neurogenic shock**: Spinal shock = temporary loss of all reflex activity below level of injury (flaccid areflexia); resolution marked by return of bulbocavernosus reflex. Neurogenic shock = hemodynamic (hypotension + bradycardia) from loss of sympathetic tone, seen in cervical/high thoracic injury
- SCIWORA (Spinal Cord Injury WithOut Radiographic Abnormality) — more common in children due to elastic ligaments/spine mobility exceeding cord tolerance
- Management principle: immobilize spine (log-roll technique), maintain MAP to preserve cord perfusion, early surgical decompression/stabilization for unstable fractures or progressive deficit; high-dose steroids (methylprednisolone) no longer routinely recommended due to lack of clear benefit and complication risk

## Ankylosing Spondylitis (Spine-Specific Points)
- See Joint Diseases file for full detail; spine-specific: sacroiliitis earliest sign, "bamboo spine" (syndesmophyte bridging) late finding, risk of spinal fracture even with minor trauma due to rigid, osteoporotic spine

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Cauda equina syndrome = surgical emergency; saddle anesthesia + urinary retention are red flags demanding urgent MRI and decompression
- L4-L5 disc herniation classically affects the L5 root (traversing nerve root theory)
- Meyerding grading of spondylolisthesis is based on percentage anterior slip (I: 0–25% through V: >100%)
- Cobb angle quantifies scoliosis severity; Risser sign assesses skeletal maturity for treatment planning
- Pott's disease classically affects the anterior vertebral body first and spares the disc initially — a key differentiator from pyogenic spondylodiscitis
- Denis classification: instability requires ≥2 of 3 columns involved; burst fractures involve anterior + middle columns
- Bulbocavernosus reflex return signals resolution of spinal shock
- Neurogenic shock (bradycardia + hypotension) must be distinguished from hypovolemic shock in a trauma patient with cervical spine injury — do not fluid-overload
