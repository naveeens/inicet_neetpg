# Pediatric Orthopedic Disorders

## Developmental Dysplasia of Hip (DDH)
### Risk Factors (mnemonic: girls, breech, first-born, family history)
- Female sex, breech presentation, first-born, positive family history, oligohydramnios, left hip more common (fetal position against maternal sacrum)

### Clinical Tests (Neonate)
| Test | Technique | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Barlow test** | Adduct + posterior pressure on flexed hip | Provokes/dislocates an unstable but located hip |
| **Ortolani test** | Abduct + anterior lift on flexed hip | Reduces a dislocated hip — palpable "clunk" |

- In older infants (>3 months): **Galeazzi sign** (unequal knee heights in flexion — apparent femoral shortening), limited hip abduction, asymmetric thigh folds
- Walking child: Trendelenburg gait, waddling gait if bilateral

### Imaging
- USG (Graf method) — investigation of choice **before 4–6 months** (femoral head not yet ossified, cartilage seen on USG not X-ray)
- X-ray becomes reliable after ossific nucleus appears (~4–6 months); look at Shenton's line (should be continuous/unbroken), acetabular index

### Management
| Age | Treatment |
|---|---|
| 0–6 months | **Pavlik harness** (dynamic flexion-abduction splinting) |
| 6–18 months | Closed reduction + spica cast (± adductor tenotomy) |
| >18 months–2 yrs | Open reduction ± femoral/pelvic osteotomy |
| Older child (walking, missed DDH) | Open reduction + osteotomy; higher complication risk |

- Major complication of treatment: **avascular necrosis of femoral head** from forceful/excessive abduction — avoid "human position" (extreme abduction); safe zone concept guides harness/cast positioning

## Congenital Talipes Equinovarus (CTEV / Clubfoot)
- Components (mnemonic **CAVE**): **C**avus, **A**dductus (forefoot), **V**arus (hindfoot), **E**quinus (ankle)
- Idiopathic (most common) vs secondary (neuromuscular — spina bifida, arthrogryposis)
- Severity assessed by **Pirani score** (clinical scoring system, 6 components, 0–6 scale)
- **Ponseti method** = treatment of choice: serial gentle manipulation + casting (correcting cavus first, then adductus/varus, equinus last) + percutaneous **tenotomy of Achilles tendon** for residual equinus + Denis-Browne boots/bar for maintenance (bracing) to prevent relapse
- Started as early as possible in neonatal period for best results

## Perthes Disease (Legg-Calvé-Perthes)
- Idiopathic avascular necrosis of the femoral head in children, typically **4–8 years**, boys > girls
- Clinical: painless/painful limp, limited hip abduction/internal rotation, may have referred pain to knee
- **Stages (Waldenström)**: Initial (sclerosis) → Fragmentation → Reossification (healing) → Remodeling (residual)
- Classification systems for prognosis:
  - **Catterall classification** (I–IV, based on % of femoral head involvement on X-ray)
  - **Herring lateral pillar classification** (A/B/B-C border/C — based on height of lateral epiphyseal pillar) — more widely used prognostically
- Key prognostic sign: **"head at risk" signs** — Gage sign, lateral subluxation, calcification lateral to epiphysis, horizontal physis, metaphyseal cyst
- Management: younger age (<6 yrs) generally good prognosis with observation/containment (abduction bracing); older children or "head at risk" hips may need surgical containment (femoral/pelvic osteotomy)

## Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE)
- Displacement of femoral epiphysis relative to metaphysis through the physis, typically **adolescents (10–16 yrs)**, obese boys most classic demographic
- Associated with obesity, hypothyroidism, renal osteodystrophy, growth hormone therapy
- Clinical: hip/groin pain, often referred to knee (important trap), limp, obligate external rotation of hip on flexion, limited internal rotation
- Radiology: **Klein's line** (line along superior femoral neck should intersect a portion of the epiphysis — fails to do so in SCFE); **Southwick angle** for quantifying severity/slip angle
- Management: **in-situ pinning/screw fixation** to prevent further slip — do NOT attempt forceful reduction (risk of avascular necrosis); contralateral hip must be monitored (high bilaterality risk)
- Major complications: avascular necrosis, chondrolysis, early OA

## Supracondylar Fracture of Humerus (Pediatric)
- Most common elbow fracture in children (peak age 5–7 years), usually extension type (fall on outstretched hand)
- **Gartland classification**:
  - Type I: undisplaced
  - Type II: displaced, posterior cortex intact (hinge)
  - Type III: completely displaced, no cortical contact
- Neurovascular structures at risk: **anterior interosseous nerve** (AIN — most commonly injured nerve overall, motor only, test OK sign/pincer), radial nerve, median nerve, and brachial artery
- Highest risk complication: **Volkmann's ischemic contracture** (from missed compartment syndrome/vascular injury) — a limb-threatening emergency
- Management: Type I — cast immobilization; Type II/III — closed reduction + percutaneous pinning (CRPP); vascular compromise needs urgent exploration

## Rickets
- Defective mineralization of growing bone due to Vitamin D deficiency (most common), also seen in renal tubular disorders (hypophosphatemic rickets), CKD
- Clinical: bow legs (genu varum), knock knees, rachitic rosary (costochondral junction widening), frontal bossing, delayed fontanelle closure, Harrison's sulcus, widened wrists/ankles, waddling gait
- Radiology: **cupping, fraying, splaying** of metaphysis (classic triad), widened growth plate, osteopenia
- Management: Vitamin D + calcium supplementation; correction of underlying disorder if secondary rickets

## Osteogenesis Imperfecta (OI)
- Genetic disorder of Type I collagen synthesis (mostly autosomal dominant, COL1A1/COL1A2 mutations)
- Clinical: multiple fragility fractures with minimal trauma, blue sclera, hearing loss, dentinogenesis imperfecta, short stature, joint hyperlaxity
- Sillence classification (Types I–IV, based on severity — Type I mildest, Type II perinatal lethal)
- Management: supportive — bisphosphonates (reduce fracture rate), fracture management, intramedullary rodding for recurrent long bone fractures, physiotherapy

## Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA)
- Arthritis of unknown cause, onset before age 16, persisting >6 weeks
- Subtypes: Systemic (Still's disease — fever, rash, arthritis, hepatosplenomegaly), Oligoarticular (most common, ANA+ association with uveitis risk), Polyarticular (RF+/RF-)
- Management: NSAIDs, DMARDs (methotrexate), biologics for refractory disease; regular ophthalmologic screening for uveitis in ANA-positive oligoarticular JIA

## Blount's Disease
- Growth disorder of proximal tibial medial physis causing progressive genu varum (bow legs)
- Infantile type (more common, <3 years, often bilateral) vs adolescent type
- Differentiate from physiological bowing (which resolves by ~2–3 years) — Blount's progresses/persists beyond this age
- **Drennan's (metaphyseal-diaphyseal) angle** on X-ray helps differentiate physiological bowing from Blount's disease
- Management: bracing in early infantile cases; corrective osteotomy for progressive/severe deformity

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Ortolani reduces a dislocated hip; Barlow provokes/dislocates an unstable hip — remember direction: Ortolani = "Out to In" (reduces)
- USG is investigation of choice for DDH before the femoral head ossifies (typically before 4–6 months); X-ray thereafter
- Ponseti method treats clubfoot in the sequence: Cavus, Adductus, Varus corrected by casting; Equinus corrected last by Achilles tenotomy
- Perthes disease = idiopathic AVN of femoral head in 4–8-year-olds; Herring lateral pillar classification guides prognosis/treatment
- SCFE classically presents with hip pain referred to the knee in an obese adolescent — do not miss this diagnosis in a child with knee pain and normal knee X-ray
- SCFE is treated by in-situ pinning; never attempt forceful reduction (risk of AVN)
- Gartland III supracondylar fracture carries highest risk for neurovascular injury and Volkmann's ischemic contracture
- Anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) is the most commonly injured nerve in supracondylar humerus fractures — purely motor, tested by "OK sign"
- Rickets shows metaphyseal cupping, fraying, and splaying on X-ray
- Blue sclera + fragility fractures + hearing loss = Osteogenesis Imperfecta (Type I collagen defect)
