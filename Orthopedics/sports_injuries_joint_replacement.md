# Sports Injuries Joint Replacement

## Knee Ligament Injuries
### Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL)
- Mechanism: sudden deceleration, pivoting/twisting on planted foot, non-contact injury common (also contact — valgus + external rotation)
- Clinical: "pop" sensation at injury, hemarthrosis (rapid swelling), instability/giving-way sensation
- Tests: **Lachman test** (most sensitive) — anterior translation of tibia at 20–30° knee flexion; **Anterior drawer test** — at 90° flexion (less sensitive than Lachman); **Pivot shift test** — reproduces instability during activity
- Management: conservative (physiotherapy, bracing) for low-demand patients; **ACL reconstruction** (using hamstring or patellar tendon autograft) for young/active/athletic patients with instability

### Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL)
- Mechanism: dashboard injury (direct blow to proximal tibia with knee flexed), hyperflexion injury
- Test: **Posterior drawer test** (most sensitive), posterior sag sign
- Often associated with multiligamentous knee injury/knee dislocation — always assess popliteal artery/peroneal nerve

### Collateral Ligaments
- **MCL (medial collateral ligament)**: valgus stress injury; tested by valgus stress test at 30° flexion; usually managed conservatively (good healing potential due to good blood supply)
- **LCL (lateral collateral ligament)**: varus stress injury; tested by varus stress test; often associated with posterolateral corner injury — higher surgical repair rate (poorer blood supply/healing potential than MCL)

### Unhappy Triad (O'Donoghue's Triad)
- Combination of **ACL + MCL + medial meniscus** injury — classic contact sports injury (valgus + external rotation with foot fixed)

## Meniscal Injuries
- Medial meniscus injured more commonly than lateral (less mobile, attached to MCL/joint capsule)
- Mechanism: twisting injury on a flexed, weight-bearing knee
- Clinical: joint line tenderness, locking (mechanical block to extension — classic for meniscal tear), clicking
- Test: **McMurray test** — pain/click with knee flexion + rotation + extension
- Investigation: MRI is gold standard non-invasive test
- Management: partial meniscectomy or meniscal repair (arthroscopic) depending on tear pattern/location (repair preferred in vascular "red zone"/peripheral tears; resection for avascular central "white zone" tears)

## Shoulder — Rotator Cuff & Impingement
- Rotator cuff: supraspinatus (most commonly torn/impinged), infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis (SITS muscles)
- Supraspinatus tendon most vulnerable due to relatively poor vascularity in the "critical zone" near insertion
- Clinical tests: **Neer impingement test**, **Hawkins-Kennedy test** (impingement); **Empty can test/Jobe test** (supraspinatus); **Lift-off test** (subscapularis); **drop arm sign** (suggests full-thickness rotator cuff tear)
- Management: conservative first (physiotherapy, NSAIDs, subacromial steroid injection) for partial tears/tendinopathy; surgical repair for full-thickness tears in active patients or failed conservative treatment

## Elbow Overuse Injuries
| Condition | Site | Mechanism | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lateral epicondylitis (Tennis elbow) | Common extensor origin (ECRB) | Repetitive wrist extension | Cozen's test (pain on resisted wrist extension) |
| Medial epicondylitis (Golfer's elbow) | Common flexor origin | Repetitive wrist flexion/pronation | Pain on resisted wrist flexion |

- Both managed conservatively initially: rest, NSAIDs, physiotherapy/eccentric strengthening, counterforce bracing; steroid injection or surgery for refractory cases

## Ankle Sprains
- Most common mechanism: inversion injury → **anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL)** most commonly injured (weakest of lateral ligament complex)
- Ottawa ankle rules help decide need for X-ray (bony tenderness at malleoli tips/navicular/5th metatarsal base, or inability to bear weight)
- Management: RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) protocol, functional bracing, early mobilization; surgery rarely needed except for chronic instability

## Terrible Triad of the Elbow
- Elbow dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid process fracture — highly unstable injury pattern requiring surgical fixation

## Total Joint Replacement (Arthroplasty)
### Indications
- End-stage osteoarthritis/rheumatoid arthritis with pain refractory to conservative management
- Avascular necrosis, post-traumatic arthritis, fracture neck of femur (displaced, elderly — hemiarthroplasty/THR)

### Total Hip Replacement (THR)
- Approaches: posterior (Moore/Southern — higher dislocation risk, especially posterior), anterolateral (Hardinge), direct anterior (lower dislocation risk, muscle-sparing, faster early recovery)
- Bearing surfaces: metal-on-polyethylene (most common), ceramic-on-ceramic, metal-on-metal (largely abandoned due to metallosis/adverse local tissue reaction)
- Post-op precautions (posterior approach): avoid hip flexion >90°, adduction past midline, and internal rotation — the classic combined movement causing posterior dislocation

### Total Knee Replacement (TKR)
- Indicated for tricompartmental knee OA/RA with pain and functional limitation
- Components: femoral component, tibial component (with polyethylene insert), ± patellar resurfacing

### Complications of Joint Replacement
| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Infection (periprosthetic joint infection) | Most feared complication; classified as early (<3 months, often intra-op contamination), delayed (3–24 months, low-virulence organisms), late (>24 months, hematogenous). Diagnosis: raised CRP/ESR, joint aspiration (cell count, culture) |
| Dislocation | Most common early complication after THR (esp. posterior approach) |
| DVT/PE | High risk after lower limb arthroplasty — routine chemoprophylaxis (LMWH/DOACs) + mechanical prophylaxis |
| Aseptic loosening | Most common cause of long-term revision surgery |
| Periprosthetic fracture | Around stem tip — intra-op or post-op fall |
| Leg length discrepancy | Common after THR — patient counseling important pre-operatively |
| Heterotopic ossification | Especially after acetabular fracture surgery/THR |

- Revision arthroplasty is more complex — indicated for aseptic loosening, infection, recurrent dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, wear/osteolysis

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Lachman test is the single most sensitive clinical test for ACL tear (more sensitive than anterior drawer)
- Unhappy triad = ACL + MCL + medial meniscus injury, classic in contact sports with valgus + external rotation force
- Medial meniscus is injured more often than lateral due to its lesser mobility and capsular/MCL attachment
- Locking of the knee (mechanical block to full extension) is a classic sign of meniscal tear
- Supraspinatus is the most commonly torn rotator cuff tendon, due to its relatively hypovascular "critical zone"
- ATFL is the most commonly injured ligament in ankle inversion sprains
- After posterior-approach THR: avoid flexion >90°, adduction, and internal rotation to prevent dislocation
- Aseptic loosening is the most common cause of late revision surgery after joint replacement; infection is the most feared early complication
- Popliteal artery and common peroneal nerve must always be assessed in significant knee dislocations (PCL-associated) given the high risk of vascular/neurological compromise
