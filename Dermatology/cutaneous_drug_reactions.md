# Cutaneous Drug Reactions

## Overview
Adverse cutaneous drug reactions (ACDRs) range from mild morbilliform exanthems to life-threatening SJS/TEN and DRESS. Recognizing severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) early and identifying/withdrawing the culprit drug is the single most important management step.

## Spectrum of Severity
| Reaction | Severity | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Morbilliform/exanthematous eruption | Mild | Most common ACDR; measles-like rash |
| Fixed drug eruption | Mild-moderate | Recurs at same site on re-exposure |
| Urticaria/angioedema | Mild-severe | Can progress to anaphylaxis |
| Erythema multiforme | Moderate | Target lesions, often infection (HSV) > drug-triggered |
| Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) | Severe | <10% BSA detachment |
| SJS-TEN overlap | Severe | 10-30% BSA detachment |
| Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN) | Life-threatening | >30% BSA detachment |
| DRESS (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms) | Life-threatening | Multi-organ involvement, delayed onset |
| AGEP (Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis) | Severe | Rapid onset sterile pustules |

## SJS/TEN

### Etiology (Drugs — high-yield culprits)
- **Sulfonamides** (co-trimoxazole)
- Anticonvulsants: **carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine, phenobarbital** (aromatic anticonvulsants)
- Allopurinol (especially in renal impairment, and in patients with **HLA-B*5801**)
- NSAIDs (oxicam group)
- Nevirapine
- Note: Also can be triggered by Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection (esp. in children)

### Pathogenesis
- Delayed-type (Type IV) hypersensitivity — cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NK cells cause widespread keratinocyte apoptosis via Fas-FasL and granulysin-mediated pathways → full-thickness epidermal necrosis and separation

### Clinical Features
- Prodrome: fever, malaise, sore throat 1-3 days before rash
- Mucosal involvement is prominent and early: oral, ocular (can cause blindness), genital erosions
- Skin: dusky-red macules/targetoid lesions → coalesce → epidermal necrolysis, sloughing in sheets
- **Nikolsky's sign positive**
- Classified by % body surface area (BSA) of epidermal detachment: SJS <10%, overlap 10-30%, TEN >30%

### Prognostic Score
**SCORTEN** — 7 criteria (age >40, malignancy, heart rate >120, initial BSA >10%, serum urea >10 mmol/L, serum bicarbonate <20 mmol/L, serum glucose >14 mmol/L); higher score = higher predicted mortality.

### Management
- Immediate withdrawal of offending drug — single most important step
- Supportive care in burns unit/ICU: fluid & electrolyte balance, wound care, nutrition, infection control, ophthalmology consult
- Role of systemic corticosteroids/IVIG/cyclosporine debated — used in select cases, especially early in course
- Avoid re-exposure — cross-reactivity within anticonvulsant classes (aromatic anticonvulsants cross-react)

## DRESS Syndrome
- Delayed onset (2-8 weeks after drug start — unlike other reactions which are faster)
- Culprits: **anticonvulsants (aromatic), allopurinol, sulfonamides, dapsone, vancomycin**
- Features: fever, morbilliform rash often progressing to erythroderma, **facial edema** (characteristic), lymphadenopathy, **eosinophilia**, atypical lymphocytes, internal organ involvement (hepatitis most common, also nephritis, myocarditis, pneumonitis)
- Associated with **HHV-6 reactivation**
- Management: stop drug, systemic corticosteroids, prolonged taper (relapses common due to slow drug clearance and viral reactivation)

## Fixed Drug Eruption
- Solitary or few, sharply demarcated, round/oval erythematous-to-violaceous plaque, may blister
- **Recurs at the exact same site** with re-exposure to the offending drug — pathognomonic feature
- Common culprits: cotrimoxazole, NSAIDs, tetracyclines
- Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation typical after resolution

## Erythema Multiforme
- Target/iris lesions (three concentric zones) — acral distribution (hands, feet, extensor surfaces)
- **Most commonly triggered by infection (HSV) rather than drugs** — important distinction from SJS/TEN which is usually drug-related
- EM minor: no/minimal mucosal involvement; EM major: significant mucosal involvement
- Self-limiting; treat underlying cause (antivirals for recurrent HSV-associated EM)

## AGEP (Acute Generalized Exanthematous Pustulosis)
- Rapid onset (within 1-2 days) of numerous small non-follicular sterile pustules on erythematous background, starting in flexures
- Fever, neutrophilia
- Culprits: beta-lactams, macrolides, calcium channel blockers, antimalarials
- Self-limiting after drug withdrawal, resolves with desquamation

## Other Notable Patterns
- **Anticoagulant (warfarin) necrosis** — due to transient Protein C deficiency early in warfarin therapy
- **Serum sickness-like reaction** — cefaclor, associated with urticaria, arthralgia, fever
- **Lichenoid drug eruption** — gold, antimalarials, thiazides
- **Photosensitivity reactions** — tetracyclines, thiazides, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, amiodarone

## High-Yield One-Liners
- SJS <10% BSA detachment, overlap 10-30%, TEN >30% — classification is purely by extent of epidermal detachment
- Aromatic anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, lamotrigine) cross-react in SJS/TEN
- Allopurinol-induced SJS/TEN strongly linked to HLA-B*5801
- DRESS has delayed onset (weeks), facial edema, eosinophilia, and HHV-6 reactivation
- Fixed drug eruption recurs at the identical site with re-exposure — pathognomonic
- Erythema multiforme is usually infection (HSV)-triggered, not drug-triggered — a classic exam distinction from SJS/TEN
- SCORTEN score predicts mortality in TEN
- First and most important step in any severe cutaneous drug reaction is prompt withdrawal of the offending drug
