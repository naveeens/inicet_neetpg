# Burns Management

## Burn Depth Classification
| Degree | Depth | Appearance | Pain | Healing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial (1st degree) | Epidermis only | Red, dry, no blisters | Painful | Heals in 3–7 days, no scarring |
| Superficial partial-thickness (2nd degree, superficial) | Epidermis + upper dermis | Red, moist, blisters | Very painful | 2–3 weeks, minimal scarring |
| Deep partial-thickness (2nd degree, deep) | Epidermis + deep dermis | Pale/mottled, may or may not blanch | Reduced sensation | 3–6 weeks, scarring common, may need grafting |
| Full-thickness (3rd degree) | Entire dermis + subcutaneous tissue | White/charred/leathery, non-blanching | Painless (nerve endings destroyed) | Does not heal without grafting |
| 4th degree | Extends to muscle/bone/tendon | Charred | Painless | Requires extensive reconstruction, often amputation |

*High-yield: A painless burn is a deep burn, not a mild one — classic exam trap. Absence of pain reflects destroyed sensory nerve endings in full-thickness burns.*

## Estimating Burn Size — Rule of Nines (Adult)
| Region | % TBSA |
|---|---|
| Head and neck | 9% |
| Each upper limb | 9% each (18% total) |
| Anterior trunk | 18% |
| Posterior trunk | 18% |
| Each lower limb | 18% each (36% total) |
| Perineum/genitalia | 1% |

- Patient's palm (including fingers) ≈ 1% TBSA — useful for small/scattered burns
- Children have proportionally larger head and smaller legs — use modified pediatric charts (Lund and Browder), not adult rule of nines

## Fluid Resuscitation
- **Parkland formula**: 4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA burned (second and third degree only) = total fluid (Ringer's lactate preferred) in first 24 h
  - Half given in first 8 hours from time of burn (not from time of arrival), remaining half over next 16 hours
- Monitor adequacy of resuscitation via **urine output** (target ~0.5 mL/kg/h in adults, ~1 mL/kg/h in children) — most reliable bedside marker
- Only burns >15–20% TBSA in adults (lower threshold in children/elderly) typically require formal IV fluid resuscitation protocols

## Airway & Inhalational Injury
- Suspect with: facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in oropharynx, hoarseness, history of enclosed-space fire
- Early intubation before airway edema progresses — do not wait for stridor
- **Carbon monoxide poisoning**: cherry-red skin (unreliable sign), normal SpO2 on pulse oximetry (pulse ox cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin — classic exam trap), treat with 100% oxygen; measure carboxyhemoglobin level directly
- Cyanide toxicity — consider in enclosed-space fires with unexplained lactic acidosis

## Escharotomy
- Indicated for circumferential full-thickness burns of limbs/chest causing vascular compromise or restricted ventilation
- Performed along mid-lateral/mid-medial lines, through eschar only (down to but not through fascia) — decompresses without formal fasciotomy unless compartment syndrome also present

## Complications
| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Curling's ulcer | Stress ulcer of stomach/duodenum in burn patients |
| Infection/sepsis | Leading cause of death after the initial resuscitation period; *Pseudomonas* classically implicated in burn wound infection |
| Hypermetabolic state | Markedly increased caloric/protein requirements — early enteral nutrition important |
| Compartment syndrome | From circumferential burns/edema — needs escharotomy/fasciotomy |
| Contractures | Late complication — physiotherapy, splinting, and early mobilization critical for prevention |
| Marjolin's ulcer | Squamous cell carcinoma arising in chronic burn scar/unstable scar tissue — years after original injury |

## Wound Management
- Silver sulfadiazine — broad-spectrum topical antimicrobial, traditional mainstay (avoid on face, deep on hands, and in G6PD deficiency/pregnancy/newborns due to sulfa component)
- Early excision and grafting for full-thickness burns — reduces infection risk and improves outcomes
- Tetanus prophylaxis for all burns per wound status

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Painless burn = full-thickness (3rd degree) burn — nerve endings destroyed, not a sign of mild injury
- Parkland formula: 4 mL/kg/%TBSA of Ringer's lactate over 24 h, half in first 8 h from time of injury
- Urine output is the best bedside indicator of adequate fluid resuscitation in burns
- Pulse oximetry is unreliable in CO poisoning (cannot differentiate carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin) — measure carboxyhemoglobin directly
- Escharotomy relieves circumferential eschar constriction; done through eschar, not fascia (unless compartment syndrome coexists)
- Marjolin's ulcer = SCC arising in chronic burn scar — long-term surveillance of old burn scars needed
- Sepsis is the leading cause of death in burn patients beyond the early resuscitation phase
