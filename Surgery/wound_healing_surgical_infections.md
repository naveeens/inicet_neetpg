# Wound Healing Surgical Infections

## Wound Healing

### Phases of Wound Healing
| Phase | Timing | Key Events |
|---|---|---|
| Hemostasis | Immediate (minutes) | Vasoconstriction, platelet plug, clot formation |
| Inflammatory | Day 0–5 | Neutrophils (early) then macrophages (key orchestrators — release growth factors); erythema, warmth, swelling |
| Proliferative | Day 3–21 | Fibroblast proliferation, **collagen (type III initially) deposition**, angiogenesis, granulation tissue formation, epithelialization |
| Remodeling/Maturation | Weeks to ~1–2 years | Type III collagen replaced by stronger **type I collagen**, collagen cross-linking, wound contraction (via myofibroblasts), scar tensile strength increases progressively but rarely exceeds ~80% of original skin strength |

*High-yield: Vitamin C is essential for collagen hydroxylation (proline/lysine hydroxylation) — deficiency (scurvy) impairs wound healing.*

### Types of Wound Healing
- **Primary intention**: clean, approximated wound edges (surgical incision) — minimal scarring, fastest healing
- **Secondary intention**: wound left open to heal by granulation and contraction (e.g., infected/contaminated wounds, pressure ulcers) — slower, more scarring
- **Tertiary intention (delayed primary closure)**: wound initially left open (e.g., to observe for infection/allow debridement), then closed surgically after a few days

### Factors Impairing Wound Healing
- Local: infection, poor blood supply/ischemia, foreign body, radiation, repeated trauma, tension on wound edges
- Systemic: diabetes mellitus, malnutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc deficiency), smoking (nicotine-induced vasoconstriction), steroids/immunosuppression, advanced age, hypoxia/anemia, obesity

### Abnormal Scarring
| Type | Key Features |
|---|---|
| Hypertrophic scar | Confined to original wound boundaries; may regress over time |
| Keloid | Extends **beyond** original wound margins; more common in darker skin types; common sites — earlobe, sternum, shoulder; tends to recur after excision alone (intralesional steroids often combined with excision) |

## Surgical Site Infections (SSI)

### Wound Classification (Predicts Infection Risk)
| Class | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Clean | No inflammation, no hollow viscus entered, no break in sterile technique | Hernia repair, thyroidectomy |
| Clean-contaminated | Hollow viscus entered under controlled conditions (GI, respiratory, GU, biliary tract) | Elective cholecystectomy, appendectomy |
| Contaminated | Major break in sterile technique, gross spillage from GI tract, penetrating trauma <4h old, acute non-purulent inflammation | Enterotomy with spillage |
| Dirty/infected | Established infection, pus, perforated viscus, old traumatic wounds with devitalized tissue | Perforated appendicitis with abscess |

### Common Causative Organisms
- *Staphylococcus aureus* — most common overall cause of SSI in clean wounds
- Gram-negative organisms (E. coli, etc.) and anaerobes — more relevant in contaminated/dirty wounds involving bowel

### Prevention
- Preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis — given within 60 minutes before incision (timing critical for efficacy), single dose usually sufficient for most clean/clean-contaminated procedures
- Skin antisepsis, appropriate hair removal (clipping, not shaving, to avoid microabrasions), maintaining normothermia and euglycemia perioperatively, minimizing OR traffic

## Surgical Site Infection Timing & Necrotizing Infections
- Most SSIs present 5–7 days postoperatively; **fever within the first 24–48 hours postoperatively is usually NOT due to SSI** — consider atelectasis (classic teaching, most common early cause), though this specific attribution has been increasingly questioned in recent literature; drug reactions and early necrotizing infections are important alternatives to exclude if fever is very early and severe
- **Necrotizing fasciitis** — rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft tissue infection; pain out of proportion to visible skin findings, crepitus, skin discoloration/bullae, systemic toxicity; polymicrobial or *Streptococcus pyogenes* (Type II, "flesh-eating") — surgical emergency requiring urgent aggressive debridement + broad-spectrum antibiotics; delay in surgery is the single biggest driver of mortality
- **Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis)** — *Clostridium perfringens*, exotoxin-mediated muscle necrosis, crepitus, gas on X-ray, foul-smelling discharge — urgent debridement + high-dose penicillin

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Type III collagen predominates early in healing; remodeled to stronger type I collagen during maturation
- Keloids extend beyond wound margins and are prone to recurrence after simple excision; hypertrophic scars stay within the wound boundary
- Vitamin C deficiency impairs collagen synthesis/hydroxylation and delays wound healing
- Clean-contaminated wounds are the classic category for elective GI/biliary/GU surgery with controlled entry into a hollow viscus
- Necrotizing fasciitis: pain out of proportion to exam findings is the classic early clue — urgent surgical debridement is life-saving
- Antibiotic prophylaxis should be given within about an hour before skin incision for maximal efficacy
- *Staphylococcus aureus* is the most common organism in surgical site infections of clean wounds
