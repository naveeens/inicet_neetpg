# Peripheral Arterial Disease

## Definition & Pathophysiology
Atherosclerotic narrowing/occlusion of peripheral arteries (most commonly lower limb) leading to reduced blood flow to the extremities. Shares risk factors with coronary and cerebrovascular atherosclerotic disease.

## Risk Factors
Smoking (strongest modifiable risk factor), diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, age, family history — essentially the same risk factor profile as coronary artery disease (PAD is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis).

## Clinical Features
| Stage | Presentation |
|---|---|
| Intermittent claudication | Cramping pain in calf/thigh/buttock on walking a consistent distance, relieved by rest — reflects the level of arterial occlusion (e.g., calf claudication = superficial femoral artery disease; buttock/thigh claudication with impotence = aortoiliac disease, **Leriche syndrome**) |
| Critical limb ischemia | Rest pain (worse at night, relieved by dangling leg over bed edge), ulceration, gangrene — limb-threatening |
| Acute limb ischemia | Sudden onset — the classic **"6 P's"**: Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Poikilothermia (coldness) — surgical emergency |

*High-yield: Leriche syndrome = triad of buttock/thigh claudication, impotence, and absent/diminished femoral pulses — due to aortoiliac occlusive disease.*

## Examination
- Diminished or absent peripheral pulses, cool extremity, pallor on elevation, dependent rubor
- Trophic changes: hair loss, skin thinning, thickened nails
- Arterial ulcers: painful, punched-out edges, typically on pressure points/toes/lateral malleolus, pale base — contrast with venous ulcers (less painful, irregular edges, gaiter area/medial malleolus, granulating base)

## Investigations
- **Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI)** — key bedside screening tool
  - Normal: ~1.0–1.4
  - PAD: <0.9
  - Severe/critical limb ischemia: <0.4
  - >1.4 suggests non-compressible/calcified vessels (common in diabetics) — falsely elevated, unreliable
- Duplex USG — first-line imaging to localize and grade stenosis
- CT angiography/MR angiography — for anatomical mapping before intervention
- Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) — gold standard, but invasive; used mainly when intervention (angioplasty) is planned simultaneously

## Management

### Chronic PAD / Intermittent Claudication
- Risk factor modification: smoking cessation (single most important intervention), statins, antiplatelet therapy (aspirin/clopidogrel), diabetes/hypertension control
- Supervised exercise/walking programs — improve claudication distance
- Cilostazol — pharmacologic option for claudication symptoms in select patients
- Revascularization (angioplasty ± stenting, or surgical bypass) reserved for lifestyle-limiting claudication refractory to conservative measures, or critical limb ischemia

### Acute Limb Ischemia
- Surgical/endovascular emergency — time-critical (irreversible muscle/nerve damage begins within hours)
- Immediate anticoagulation (IV heparin) while arranging intervention
- Embolic occlusion → embolectomy (Fogarty catheter); thrombotic occlusion on background atherosclerosis → thrombolysis or bypass surgery depending on severity/viability
- Assess viability — if limb non-viable (fixed mottling, absent Doppler signals, dense paralysis) → primary amputation rather than futile revascularization attempt

## Complications
- Non-healing ulcers, gangrene, limb loss (amputation)
- Reperfusion injury after revascularization of acutely/severely ischemic limb → **compartment syndrome**, myoglobinuria/acute kidney injury (rhabdomyolysis), hyperkalemia — fasciotomy may be needed
- Systemic atherosclerosis — PAD patients carry significantly elevated risk of coronary and cerebrovascular events; long-term mortality is often driven by cardiovascular disease rather than the limb itself

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Leriche syndrome: buttock/thigh claudication + impotence + absent femoral pulses = aortoiliac occlusive disease
- Acute limb ischemia = the "6 P's" — pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia — a surgical emergency
- ABI <0.9 defines PAD; ABI <0.4 indicates critical limb ischemia; ABI >1.4 suggests falsely elevated readings from calcified non-compressible vessels (common in diabetics)
- Smoking cessation is the single most impactful modifiable intervention in PAD
- Arterial ulcers are painful with punched-out edges on pressure points; venous ulcers are less painful with irregular edges in the gaiter area
- Reperfusion after revascularizing a severely ischemic limb can precipitate compartment syndrome and myoglobinuric renal injury
- PAD is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis — patients need aggressive cardiovascular risk factor management, not just limb-focused therapy
