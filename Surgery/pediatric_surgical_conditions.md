# Pediatric Surgical Conditions

## Congenital Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis (CHPS)
- Hypertrophy of pyloric circular muscle → gastric outlet obstruction
- Presentation: **non-bilious projectile vomiting** at **2–8 weeks of age**, classically first-born male infants, hungry after vomiting
- Palpable **"olive-shaped" mass** in epigastrium/right upper quadront; visible gastric peristalsis
- Metabolic derangement: **hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis** with paradoxical aciduria (from vomiting of gastric HCl and renal potassium/hydrogen wasting to conserve sodium)
- Diagnosis: USG abdomen — pyloric muscle thickness >3–4 mm, canal length >15–17 mm (verify exact cutoffs against current radiology reference, as measurement thresholds vary slightly by source)
- Management: correct fluid/electrolyte imbalance FIRST (not emergency surgery) → then **Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy**

*High-yield: CHPS is a medical emergency for correction of alkalosis/dehydration, NOT a surgical emergency — surgery is done only after metabolic correction.*

## Intussusception
- Telescoping of proximal bowel segment into distal segment; most common cause of intestinal obstruction in children **6 months–2 years**
- Most commonly ileocolic
- Idiopathic in most infants (preceding viral infection/Peyer's patch hypertrophy theorized); in older children, look for a pathological lead point (Meckel's diverticulum, polyp, lymphoma)
- Classic triad: colicky abdominal pain (episodic, drawing legs up), vomiting, **"red currant jelly" stools** (blood + mucus — a late sign)
- **Sausage-shaped abdominal mass**, often palpable
- Diagnosis: USG — **"target sign"/"doughnut sign"** on cross-section
- Management: **air/contrast enema reduction** — diagnostic and therapeutic (first-line if no signs of peritonitis/perforation); surgery if reduction fails or peritonitis/perforation present

## Hirschsprung's Disease (Congenital Aganglionic Megacolon)
- Absence of ganglion cells (Meissner's and Auerbach's plexus) in distal bowel segment due to failure of neural crest cell migration → functional obstruction
- Presentation: delayed passage of meconium (**>48 h** in a term neonate), abdominal distension, bilious vomiting; chronic constipation if diagnosed later in life
- Rectosigmoid most commonly involved (short-segment disease most common)
- Diagnosis: **rectal biopsy** (absence of ganglion cells) = gold standard; barium enema shows transition zone (narrow aganglionic distal segment → dilated proximal normal bowel)
- Management: initial decompression, definitive treatment = surgical resection of aganglionic segment with pull-through procedure (e.g., Swenson, Duhamel, or Soave procedure)
- Feared complication: **Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis** — life-threatening, can occur pre- or post-operatively

## Anorectal Malformations (Imperforate Anus)
- Spectrum from low (perineal fistula) to high (rectourethral/rectovesical fistula) lesions
- Associated with **VACTERL** association (Vertebral, Anal, Cardiac, Tracheoesophageal, Renal, Limb anomalies) — screen for associated defects
- Invertogram (historically) / cross-table lateral radiograph — assesses level of the pouch relative to the pubococcygeal line to guide management
- Management depends on level: low lesions — primary perineal anoplasty; high lesions — staged approach (colostomy → definitive pull-through, e.g., posterior sagittal anorectoplasty → colostomy closure)

## Esophageal Atresia / Tracheoesophageal Fistula (TEF)
- Most common type: **esophageal atresia with distal TEF** (blind-ending proximal esophageal pouch, fistula from distal esophageal segment to trachea)
- Presentation: excessive drooling/frothing, choking/cyanosis with feeds, inability to pass NG tube (coils in proximal pouch), polyhydramnios antenatally
- Associated with **VACTERL** association
- Diagnosis: failure to pass NG/orogastric tube, confirmed by X-ray showing tube coiled in upper pouch; gasless abdomen suggests pure atresia without distal fistula, gas-filled abdomen suggests distal TEF present
- Management: surgical — primary repair (ligation of fistula + primary esophageal anastomosis) in stable infants; staged repair (gastrostomy first) if long-gap atresia or unstable infant

## Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia (CDH)
- Most common type: **Bochdalek hernia** (posterolateral defect, usually left-sided) — abdominal viscera herniate into thoracic cavity during development
- Leads to pulmonary hypoplasia and persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn — the main determinants of survival, not the hernia repair itself
- Presentation: respiratory distress at birth, scaphoid abdomen, bowel sounds in chest, displaced heart sounds
- Management: immediate resuscitation/intubation (avoid bag-mask ventilation — distends bowel in chest, worsens compromise), NG decompression, delayed surgical repair after physiological stabilization (not an immediate surgical emergency — physiologic stabilization takes priority)

## Undescended Testis (Cryptorchidism)
- Failure of testicular descent into scrotum; most common in premature infants
- Most undescended testes at birth descend spontaneously by 6 months of age (after which further spontaneous descent is unlikely)
- Risk if uncorrected: infertility, increased risk of testicular malignancy (in both undescended and contralateral testis), torsion, inguinal hernia
- Management: **orchidopexy**, per current recommendations generally performed around 6–18 months of age if testis has not descended spontaneously (verify exact timing against current pediatric urology guideline)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- CHPS: correct metabolic alkalosis and dehydration BEFORE pyloromyotomy — not a surgical emergency
- Intussusception classic triad: colicky pain, vomiting, red-currant-jelly stool; USG shows target/doughnut sign
- Hirschsprung's disease: delayed meconium passage >48h, diagnosed by rectal biopsy showing absent ganglion cells
- VACTERL association links anorectal malformations and TEF/esophageal atresia — screen for associated anomalies
- Esophageal atresia: inability to pass NG tube + coiling on X-ray is diagnostic
- CDH survival is determined more by degree of pulmonary hypoplasia/hypertension than by the anatomical defect itself; avoid bag-mask ventilation
- Orchidopexy reduces but does not eliminate future malignancy risk in cryptorchidism
