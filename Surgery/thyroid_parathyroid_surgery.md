# Thyroid Parathyroid Surgery

## Thyroid Nodule Evaluation
- Clinical assessment + TSH first
- USG — characterizes nodule (solid vs cystic, suspicious features: microcalcifications, irregular margins, hypoechogenicity, increased vascularity)
- **FNAC** — investigation of choice for thyroid nodules; Bethesda system for reporting cytology (categories I–VI, guiding management from benign/observation to malignant/surgery)
- Radionuclide scan: reserved for cases with suppressed TSH (to identify "hot"/autonomous nodules, which are rarely malignant and usually don't need FNAC)

## Thyroid Carcinoma
| Type | Key Features |
|---|---|
| Papillary carcinoma | **Most common thyroid cancer overall**; excellent prognosis; spreads via lymphatics; **psammoma bodies**, "Orphan Annie eye" nuclei on histology; associated with childhood radiation exposure |
| Follicular carcinoma | Second most common; spreads hematogenously (bone, lung); cannot be diagnosed on FNAC alone — needs histology to assess capsular/vascular invasion (distinguishes from follicular adenoma) |
| Medullary carcinoma | Arises from **parafollicular C-cells** — secretes **calcitonin** (tumor marker); associated with **MEN 2A/2B**, RET proto-oncogene mutation; screen family members |
| Anaplastic carcinoma | Most aggressive, elderly, rapidly enlarging neck mass, poor prognosis, often unresectable at presentation |
| Lymphoma | Associated with Hashimoto's thyroiditis background |

*High-yield: Papillary carcinoma spreads via lymphatics (cervical lymph nodes) yet still has the best prognosis among thyroid cancers — a classic exam paradox.*

### Management
- Surgery is mainstay: total/near-total thyroidectomy for most differentiated thyroid cancers >1cm (lobectomy may suffice for small, low-risk papillary carcinomas per current risk-stratified guidelines — verify exact size cutoffs against latest guideline)
- Radioactive iodine (I-131) ablation post-thyroidectomy for differentiated (papillary/follicular) carcinoma — relies on iodine uptake by residual thyroid tissue
- TSH suppression therapy (levothyroxine) post-thyroidectomy — reduces recurrence in differentiated cancers
- Medullary carcinoma: does NOT take up radioiodine (arises from C-cells, not follicular cells) — surgery is primary treatment; monitor calcitonin/CEA for recurrence
- Anaplastic carcinoma: largely palliative; surgery rarely curative given late presentation

## Goiter
- Simple/multinodular goiter — usually iodine deficiency related or physiological in endemic areas
- Toxic multinodular goiter (Plummer's disease) — autonomous nodules causing hyperthyroidism
- Retrosternal extension, tracheal compression (stridor), or cosmetic/compressive symptoms → surgical indication

## Hyperthyroidism (Surgical Relevance)
- **Graves' disease** — most common cause of hyperthyroidism; autoimmune, TSH receptor antibodies; diffuse goiter, exophthalmos, pretibial myxedema
- Management: antithyroid drugs (carbimazole/methimazole, propylthiouracil — preferred in first trimester of pregnancy), radioactive iodine, or surgery (subtotal/total thyroidectomy)
- **Thyroid storm**: life-threatening exacerbation — high fever, tachycardia, altered mentation; treat with beta-blockers, PTU/methimazole, iodine (Lugol's/SSKI — given after antithyroid drugs to avoid Jod-Basedow effect), steroids, cooling

## Thyroidectomy Complications
| Complication | Key Points |
|---|---|
| Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury | Hoarseness (unilateral); bilateral injury → airway obstruction (medical emergency, may need tracheostomy) |
| External laryngeal nerve (branch of superior laryngeal) injury | Loss of voice projection/high pitch (affects cricothyroid muscle) — subtle, often missed |
| Hypoparathyroidism | Inadvertent removal/devascularization of parathyroids → hypocalcemia — Chvostek's sign, Trousseau's sign, perioral tingling |
| Hemorrhage/hematoma | Can cause airway compromise — watch for neck swelling, respiratory distress post-op |
| Thyroid storm (if unprepared hyperthyroid patient operated) | Preoperative euthyroid state is essential before elective thyroid surgery in hyperthyroid patients |

## Parathyroid Disease

### Primary Hyperparathyroidism
- Most common cause: **parathyroid adenoma** (single gland, ~80–85%); also hyperplasia (often 4-gland, associated with MEN syndromes), rarely carcinoma
- Presentation: **"Bones, stones, abdominal groans, psychiatric moans"** — bone pain/fractures (osteitis fibrosa cystica), renal stones, abdominal pain/pancreatitis/PUD, depression/psychiatric symptoms; often now diagnosed incidentally via routine calcium testing (asymptomatic hypercalcemia)
- Labs: ↑ serum calcium, ↑ PTH (inappropriately normal/high for calcium level), ↓ phosphate
- Localization: USG neck, **sestamibi scan** (preferred nuclear imaging for parathyroid adenoma localization)
- Treatment: surgical parathyroidectomy — definitive for symptomatic disease/significant hypercalcemia

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Papillary carcinoma = most common thyroid cancer, spreads via lymphatics, best prognosis despite nodal spread
- Follicular carcinoma spreads hematogenously; cannot be diagnosed on FNAC alone (needs capsule/vascular invasion assessment)
- Medullary carcinoma secretes calcitonin, arises from C-cells, associated with MEN 2, does not take up radioiodine
- Bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve injury during thyroidectomy is an airway emergency
- Sestamibi scan is the imaging of choice to localize parathyroid adenomas
- "Bones, stones, abdominal groans, psychiatric moans" = classic (though now often subclinical) presentation of primary hyperparathyroidism
- Thyroid storm requires antithyroid drugs BEFORE iodine to prevent Jod-Basedow phenomenon (iodine-induced thyrotoxicosis)
