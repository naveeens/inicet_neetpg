# Urology Stones UTI

## Urolithiasis (Renal/Ureteric Stones)

### Types of Stones
| Stone Type | Frequency | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate | Most common (~75–80%) | Radiopaque; associated with hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria; can be idiopathic |
| Calcium phosphate | Common | Radiopaque; associated with renal tubular acidosis (RTA type 1), hyperparathyroidism |
| Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) | Associated with UTI | Formed by urease-producing organisms (**Proteus, Klebsiella**); can form large **"staghorn calculi"**; radiopaque |
| Uric acid stones | ~5–10% | **Radiolucent** (not seen on plain X-ray, seen on CT/USG) — associated with gout, high purine diet, acidic urine |
| Cystine stones | Rare | Hereditary (cystinuria — defective renal tubular reabsorption of cystine); radiopaque (faintly), hexagonal crystals on urine microscopy |

*High-yield: Uric acid stones are radiolucent — a classic exam differentiator; all other major stone types are radiopaque on plain X-ray.*

### Clinical Features
- Renal colic: severe, colicky flank pain radiating to groin ("loin to groin"), patient writhes in agony (unlike peritonitis where patient lies still)
- Hematuria (micro or macroscopic), nausea/vomiting, dysuria/urgency if stone at vesicoureteric junction
- Fever with obstruction → suspect infected obstructed system (pyonephrosis) — urological emergency requiring urgent decompression

### Diagnosis
- **Non-contrast CT (CT-KUB)** — gold standard, most sensitive and specific for stones of any composition
- USG — first-line in pregnancy/children (avoids radiation), detects hydronephrosis, may miss small/ureteric stones
- Plain X-ray KUB — detects radiopaque stones only

### Management
| Stone Size | Approach |
|---|---|
| <5–6 mm | High likelihood of spontaneous passage; conservative management (hydration, analgesia, alpha-blockers like tamsulosin to aid passage) |
| 6–10 mm | Trial of conservative management vs intervention depending on symptoms/location |
| >10 mm or failed conservative trial | Intervention needed — **ESWL** (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy), **ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy**, or **PCNL** (percutaneous nephrolithotomy, preferred for large/staghorn/renal pelvis stones) |
| Infected obstructed system | Emergency decompression (nephrostomy or ureteric stent) — definitive stone treatment deferred until infection controlled |

## Urinary Tract Infection (Surgical Relevance)
- Most common organism: *E. coli* (uncomplicated UTI); complicated/catheter-associated UTIs — broader spectrum (Klebsiella, Proteus, Pseudomonas, Enterococcus)
- Recurrent UTI, especially with underlying stones/obstruction/structural anomaly → warrants urological workup (imaging to rule out stone, obstruction, reflux)
- Pyonephrosis (infected hydronephrosis) — urological emergency, requires urgent drainage + antibiotics

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Calcium oxalate = most common stone type overall; uric acid = radiolucent (classic exam differentiator)
- Struvite stones form staghorn calculi and are associated with urease-producing organisms (Proteus)
- CT-KUB (non-contrast) is the gold standard investigation for urolithiasis
- Loin-to-groin colicky pain with patient writhing = renal colic (contrast with peritonitis, where patient lies still)
- Infected obstructed kidney (pyonephrosis) needs emergency decompression before definitive stone management
- Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) aid spontaneous passage of small ureteric stones
- PCNL is preferred for large renal pelvis/staghorn calculi; ESWL best for smaller stones with favorable location
