# Urologic Oncology

## Prostate Cancer

### Epidemiology & Risk Factors
- Common malignancy in older men; risk increases with age, family history, African ancestry (per international epidemiologic data); androgen-dependent growth
- Arises predominantly from the **peripheral zone** — often palpable on DRE, may be asymptomatic until locally advanced or metastatic

### Screening & Diagnosis
- **PSA** — widely used but imperfect (not cancer-specific; also elevated in BPH, prostatitis, after DRE/ejaculation/instrumentation); screening strategy (routine PSA-based screening vs shared decision-making) varies by guideline body and has evolved — verify against current guideline for exam purposes
- DRE — hard, nodular, irregular prostate, loss of median sulcus
- Multiparametric MRI increasingly used to guide biopsy
- Transrectal USG-guided biopsy — definitive diagnosis
- **Gleason score/grade group** — histological grading, predicts aggressiveness and guides management

### Staging & Spread
- Local spread: seminal vesicles, bladder base
- Lymphatic spread: obturator, iliac nodes
- Hematogenous spread: **bone (classically osteoblastic/sclerotic metastases** — contrast with most other cancers causing osteolytic lesions), especially axial skeleton (spine, pelvis)

### Management
| Stage | Approach |
|---|---|
| Localized, low-risk, especially elderly/comorbid | Active surveillance (given slow-growing nature in many cases) |
| Localized, higher-risk, good life expectancy | Radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy (external beam/brachytherapy) — comparable outcomes, choice individualized |
| Locally advanced | Radiotherapy + androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) |
| Metastatic | ADT (LHRH agonists/antagonists, orchiectomy) ± chemotherapy/newer androgen-pathway inhibitors |

*High-yield: Prostate cancer metastases to bone are classically osteoblastic (sclerotic) on imaging, unlike the osteolytic pattern typical of most other metastatic cancers — frequently tested distinction.*

## Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)
- Most common renal malignancy in adults; arises from renal tubular epithelium (proximal tubule); clear cell type most common histology
- Risk factors: smoking, obesity, hypertension, dialysis-associated acquired cystic disease, von Hippel-Lindau syndrome (associated with bilateral/multiple RCCs at younger age)
- Classic triad (uncommon at presentation now, often found incidentally on imaging): flank pain, hematuria, palpable abdominal mass
- **Paraneoplastic syndromes**: erythrocytosis (ectopic EPO), hypercalcemia (PTHrP), hypertension (renin), Stauffer syndrome (non-metastatic hepatic dysfunction)
- Left-sided RCC classically causes left varicocele (via left renal vein/gonadal vein compression) that does not disappear on lying down — concerning sign in an adult male, warrants renal imaging
- Spreads via renal vein → IVC (can form tumor thrombus extending into right atrium) — imaging must assess venous extension before surgery
- Management: partial or radical nephrectomy depending on tumor size/location; targeted therapy/immunotherapy for metastatic disease

## Bladder Cancer
- **Transitional cell (urothelial) carcinoma** — most common type in most populations
- Squamous cell carcinoma — associated with chronic irritation, **schistosomiasis** (endemic areas), chronic catheterization, bladder stones
- Strongest risk factor: **smoking**; also occupational exposure to aromatic amines (dye, rubber industry)
- **Painless gross hematuria** — classic presenting symptom, must always be investigated in adults (never dismiss as UTI without workup)
- Diagnosis: cystoscopy + biopsy/TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumor) — diagnostic and often therapeutic for non-muscle-invasive disease
- Management: TURBT ± intravesical BCG/chemotherapy for non-muscle-invasive disease; radical cystectomy ± neoadjuvant chemotherapy for muscle-invasive disease

## Testicular Cancer
- Most common solid malignancy in young men (15–35 years)
- Risk factor: **cryptorchidism** (undescended testis) — risk reduced but not eliminated by orchidopexy; also increases risk in the contralateral normally descended testis
- Types: **Germ cell tumors** (~95%) — seminoma (most common single type, radiosensitive, better prognosis) vs non-seminomatous (embryonal, yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, teratoma — often mixed, more aggressive)
- Tumor markers: **AFP** (yolk sac elements), **β-hCG** (choriocarcinoma/some embryonal), **LDH** (non-specific, marker of tumor burden) — pure seminoma typically has normal AFP
- Presentation: painless testicular swelling/mass (painful presentation can mimic epididymo-orchitis/torsion — imaging and index of suspicion needed)
- Management: radical inguinal orchidectomy (never transscrotal biopsy — risk of altering lymphatic drainage/seeding) followed by risk-adapted surveillance, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy depending on histology and stage

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Prostate cancer bone metastases are classically osteoblastic (sclerotic), unlike most cancers which cause osteolytic lesions
- RCC classic triad (flank pain, hematuria, mass) is now uncommonly seen together — most RCCs are found incidentally
- Left-sided varicocele that doesn't reduce on lying flat in an adult → rule out left renal cell carcinoma (renal vein involvement)
- Painless gross hematuria in an adult = investigate for bladder cancer until proven otherwise
- Testicular cancer diagnosis and treatment starts with radical inguinal orchidectomy — never a transscrotal biopsy
- Seminoma is radiosensitive with generally normal AFP; elevated AFP suggests a non-seminomatous component
- Cryptorchidism increases testicular cancer risk in both the undescended and the contralateral normally descended testis
