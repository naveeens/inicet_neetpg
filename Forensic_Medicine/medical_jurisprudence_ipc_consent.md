# Medical Jurisprudence IPC Consent

## Important Note on Current Law — IPC vs BNS
India's principal criminal code, the **Indian Penal Code (IPC), 1860**, has been **replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023**, effective **1 July 2024**. Most standard forensic medicine textbooks (Reddy's, etc.) and older exam question banks still reference **IPC section numbers**, and these classic numbers remain extremely high-yield to know because that is how most exam questions (and even many current teaching materials) are still framed. However, **the section numbers themselves have changed under BNS** — this file gives the classic IPC numbers (what you need to recognize/recall for most exam banks) and flags each one that you should **verify against the current BNS section number** before relying on it for a live legal/clinical scenario, since the renumbering is a recent and still-settling detail.

## Definitions
- **Medical Jurisprudence (Forensic Medicine)**: application of medical knowledge to the purposes of law.
- **Medical Ethics**: moral principles governing the practice of medicine — professional conduct, etiquette, and duties to patients, colleagues, and society.
- **Consent**: voluntary agreement by a person (with capacity to understand and decide) to what is being done to their own body.

## Types of Consent
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Implied consent** | Inferred from a patient's actions/circumstances (e.g., extending an arm for a blood draw, attending OPD) — covers only routine, non-invasive examination |
| **Expressed consent** | Explicitly stated — oral or written |
| **Informed consent** | Expressed consent given after the patient has been informed of the diagnosis, nature and purpose of the procedure, material risks/benefits, and alternatives (including no treatment) — the current legal and ethical standard for any invasive procedure/surgery/research |

## Valid Consent — Requirements
For consent to be legally valid, the person must:
1. Have the legal capacity/competence to consent (age, mental soundness, not intoxicated)
2. Consent voluntarily, free from fraud, fear, or misconception of fact
3. Have adequate information/understanding of what is being consented to (informed consent)

## IPC Provisions on Consent (Chapter IV — General Exceptions) — classic numbers
| Section (IPC) | Provision | Verify BNS equivalent? |
|---|---|---|
| **Section 87** | Consent by a person above 18 years to an act not intended, and not known, to cause death — done in good faith, without intent to cause harm (e.g., sports, minor procedures) | Yes — renumbered under BNS General Exceptions chapter |
| **Section 88** | Act done in good faith for a person's benefit, with their consent, even if it carries a risk (covers surgery/medical treatment performed with consent, in good faith, for the patient's benefit) | Yes |
| **Section 89** | Consent by or on behalf of a child under 12 years, or a person of unsound mind, given by a guardian/lawful custodian, for an act done in good faith for that person's benefit | Yes |
| **Section 90** | Defines what is **NOT valid consent**: consent given under fear of injury or misconception of fact (and the person doing the act knows/has reason to believe this); consent of a person of unsound mind or under intoxication who is unable to understand the nature of the act; consent of a child under 12 years | Yes |
| **Section 91** | Exclusion of acts which are offences independently of harm caused (consent to one act does not excuse an independently punishable offence) | Yes |
| **Section 92** | Act done in good faith for a person's benefit **without consent**, where circumstances make it impossible for the person to signify consent, or there is no time to obtain consent from a guardian (e.g., emergency treatment of an unconscious patient) | Yes |

*High-yield: Consent under Section 90 obtained by putting the person in fear of death or grievous hurt is NOT valid consent — this is directly relevant to rape law (consent obtained under fear/threat is not real consent) and to consent taken by misrepresentation for medical procedures.*

## Age Thresholds for Consent — Frequently Tested
| Context | Age |
|---|---|
| Consent to medical/surgical treatment for one's own benefit under IPC S.88 (act in good faith, not intending death) | Adult (implied ≥12 per S.89 read with S.90 as the floor below which consent is invalid) |
| Consent invalid if given by a child under this age (IPC S.90) | **12 years** |
| Guardian's consent valid on behalf of child under this age (IPC S.89) | **Under 12 years** |
| Age of consent for sexual intercourse (statutory rape threshold) | **18 years** (as per Criminal Law Amendment 2013 and POCSO Act, 2012 — even a "consenting" partner below 18 does not provide valid legal consent) |
| Age at which minor can independently consent to medical termination of pregnancy (MTP Act) | Guardian consent required if the woman is a minor (<18 years) or mentally ill; woman's own consent suffices if ≥18 and mentally competent |

## Medical Negligence
- **Civil negligence**: breach of a duty of care owed to the patient, causing damage — remedy is compensation; doctors/hospitals are liable under the **Consumer Protection Act** (medical services held to be a "service" — landmark: *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha*).
- **Criminal negligence**: gross/rash negligence causing death — IPC **Section 304A** ("causing death by negligence," classic teaching; note this section has been widely reported as renumbered under BNS — verify current BNS section number, commonly cited around BNS Section 106, before quoting it as current law).
- **Bolam test**: standard of care is judged by what a reasonably competent professional in that field would have done — a doctor is not negligent if acting in accordance with a practice accepted by a responsible body of medical opinion, even if another body of opinion differs.
- **Res ipsa loquitur** ("the thing speaks for itself"): negligence presumed from the very nature of the event (e.g., a surgical instrument left inside the body) without need for further proof of the standard of care breached.
- **Contributory negligence**: patient's own conduct (e.g., not following instructions) contributed to the harm — may reduce liability.

## Good Samaritan Provisions
- Legal protection (under Indian law and Supreme Court/Ministry of Health guidelines) for bystanders/doctors who render emergency assistance in good faith to accident/injury victims, shielding them from civil/criminal liability and harassment (e.g., not being compelled to become witnesses, no requirement to pay for treatment before police formalities).

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Consent under fear of injury or misconception of fact (IPC S.90) is **not valid consent** — classic exam trap for both surgical consent and rape law questions.
- A child under 12 years cannot give valid consent (IPC S.90); a guardian may consent on the child's behalf for their benefit (IPC S.89).
- Age of consent for sexual intercourse in India is **18 years**, regardless of any "consent" given by the minor.
- Bolam test = standard of care judged against a reasonably competent peer body of medical opinion.
- Res ipsa loquitur = negligence presumed from the nature of the incident itself (e.g., retained surgical instrument).
- IPC is the classic textbook/exam-bank reference, but **current Indian law is the BNS (effective 1 July 2024)** — always double-check whether a question wants the "classic IPC number" or the "current BNS number," and verify the BNS mapping before citing it as settled fact.
