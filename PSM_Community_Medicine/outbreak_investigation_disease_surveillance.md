# Outbreak Investigation Disease Surveillance

## Definitions
- **Outbreak/Epidemic**: occurrence of disease cases in excess of normal expectancy for a given time and place
- **Endemic**: habitual/usual presence of disease in a population/area
- **Pandemic**: epidemic spread across multiple countries/continents affecting a large number of people
- **Sporadic**: irregular, scattered cases occurring occasionally with no discernible pattern

## Steps in Outbreak Investigation (Classic Sequence — high-yield order)
1. Verify the diagnosis (confirm cases are truly the suspected disease — lab confirmation)
2. Confirm the existence of an outbreak (compare with expected baseline for that time/place)
3. Define a **case definition** (clinical criteria + time + place + person) and count cases
4. Describe the outbreak epidemiologically — **time, place, person** (descriptive epidemiology)
5. Develop a hypothesis regarding source and mode of spread
6. Test the hypothesis (analytical study — usually case-control for outbreak setting, quick to conduct)
7. Institute control and preventive measures (do NOT wait until investigation is complete — implement as soon as source/mode is reasonably suspected)
8. Evaluate efficacy of control measures
9. Communicate findings (report writing, feedback to authorities/community)

*High-yield: Control measures should be instituted as soon as a probable cause is identified — do not delay control for the sake of completing the investigation.*

## Epidemic Curve (Epi Curve)
| Pattern | Shape | Suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Point source | Sharp rise, sharp fall, single incubation-period width | Single common exposure (e.g., contaminated food at one event) |
| Continuous common source | Rises and plateaus over a period, may have irregular peaks | Ongoing exposure to same source (e.g., contaminated water supply) |
| Propagated (person-to-person) | Series of progressively larger peaks, each ~1 incubation period apart | Communicable disease spreading through contact |
| Mixed | Point source followed by secondary propagated wave | Initial common source plus secondary transmission |

## Descriptive Epidemiology — Person, Place, Time
- **Person**: age, sex, occupation, immunization status — identifies at-risk groups
- **Place**: spot maps, geographic clustering — identifies source location
- **Time**: epidemic curve — identifies incubation period, mode of spread, single vs continuing exposure

## Disease Surveillance
- **Surveillance**: continuous, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data essential for planning, implementation, and evaluation of public health action
- **Passive surveillance**: routine reporting by health facilities without active case search (cheap but underestimates true burden)
- **Active surveillance**: health department actively seeks out cases (more sensitive, resource-intensive) — used for disease elimination/eradication programs
- **Sentinel surveillance**: monitoring disease trends in selected representative sites/population rather than the whole population — cost-effective for trend detection

## Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP)
- India's programme for strengthening disease surveillance for epidemic-prone diseases at district, state, and national levels
- Reporting formats: **"S" (Suspected case, by health worker), "P" (Presumptive/probable, by clinician), "L" (Laboratory-confirmed, by lab)** — per current knowledge, verify against latest program guideline for exact nomenclature/formats
- Involves weekly reporting; rapid response teams (RRT) for outbreak response

*Per current knowledge — verify exact reporting formats, unit names, and current administrative structure against the latest IDSP/National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) guideline, as surveillance programs are periodically restructured.*

## Notifiable Diseases
- Diseases that must be reported to public health authorities by law (varies by state in India)
- Purpose: early detection of outbreaks, monitoring trends, guiding control policy
- Examples classically include: cholera, plague, diphtheria, measles, acute flaccid paralysis (AFP), and others per state/national list — verify current list against latest Indian Public Health Act/notification, as these are periodically updated

## Investigation of a Specific Outbreak — Food/Waterborne
- Rapid onset with short incubation → toxin-mediated (e.g., Staph aureus enterotoxin, Bacillus cereus)
- Longer incubation → infectious agent (e.g., Salmonella, Vibrio cholerae, Hepatitis A/E for water source)
- Case-control study preferred in outbreak setting (quick, doesn't require knowing full at-risk population — useful when the outbreak population/denominator is not well defined, e.g., at a wedding or function)
- Cohort study preferred when the exposed population/denominator is well defined (e.g., all attendees of a specific event with a guest list)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- First step in outbreak investigation: verify/confirm the diagnosis, not immediately jump to control measures
- Control measures should start as soon as source is reasonably suspected — do not wait for investigation to conclude
- Point-source epidemic curve = single sharp peak; propagated curve = successive rising peaks each an incubation period apart
- Passive surveillance is basic/routine; active surveillance is more sensitive but resource-intensive; sentinel surveillance monitors trends via representative sites
- Case-control studies are typically preferred over cohort in outbreak settings when the denominator/population at risk is undefined
- Epidemic curve time axis, when combined with the incubation period, helps estimate the likely point of exposure
