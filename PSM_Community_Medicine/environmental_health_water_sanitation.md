# Environmental Health Water Sanitation

## Water Purification — Stages
1. **Storage** — self-purification (sedimentation, oxidation, sunlight/UV action reduce bacterial load over time)
2. **Filtration** — removes suspended particles and a large fraction of bacteria
   - **Slow sand filter**: uses biological layer ("schmutzdecke") for purification; removes ~99% bacteria; requires larger area, less frequent cleaning
   - **Rapid sand filter**: uses coagulation (alum) pre-treatment + mechanical filtration; requires chlorination afterward for complete disinfection; smaller area, needs frequent backwashing
3. **Disinfection** — chlorination is the standard method for large-scale municipal water disinfection

## Chlorination
- **Breakpoint chlorination**: chlorine dose at which free residual chlorine appears after satisfying all chlorine demand (organic matter, ammonia) — represents the ideal disinfection dose
- **Free residual chlorine**: 0.2–0.5 mg/L maintained at the point of consumption after a contact period of at least 30 minutes — ensures continued protection against contamination in the distribution system (verify exact figures against current WHO/BIS standard)
- **Superchlorination**: deliberately adding excess chlorine (beyond breakpoint) for rapid disinfection in emergencies (e.g., after floods, outbreaks) — followed by **dechlorination** before the water is used, if taste/odor is objectionable
- Chlorine demand = amount of chlorine consumed by organic matter, ammonia, and other reducing substances in water before residual chlorine appears

## Water Quality Testing
- **Bacteriological indicator**: presence of **coliform organisms** (especially *E. coli*, indicator of fecal contamination) — water for drinking should ideally show **0 coliforms/100 mL**
- **Chemical indicators**: pH, hardness, chlorides, nitrates, fluoride, arsenic — each has recommended safe limits per BIS/WHO standards (verify current specific values)
- **Physical**: turbidity, color, odor, taste

## Fluoride and Water
- **Fluorosis**: excess fluoride in drinking water (endemic in several regions of India) causes dental fluorosis (mottled enamel) at lower excess levels and skeletal fluorosis at higher/chronic excess
- Deficient fluoride is associated with increased dental caries — fluoridation of water supply used in some countries as a public health measure (not a standard universal Indian program practice; India predominantly faces excess fluoride issues in specific endemic belts)

## Waterborne Diseases
| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Bacterial | Cholera, Typhoid, Bacillary dysentery (Shigella), E. coli diarrhea |
| Viral | Hepatitis A, Hepatitis E, Rotavirus diarrhea, Poliomyelitis |
| Protozoal | Amoebiasis, Giardiasis |
| Helminthic | Ascariasis, Guinea worm (dracunculiasis — India declared free) |
| Chemical | Fluorosis, Arsenicosis, Methemoglobinemia (nitrate contamination in infants) |

## Sanitation and Excreta Disposal
| Method | Setting | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Sanitary latrine (pour-flush/pit) | Rural, promoted under Swachh Bharat Mission | Prevents fecal-oral contamination, breaks disease transmission cycle |
| Septic tank | Individual households/small institutions without sewer connection | Anaerobic digestion of sewage on-site |
| Underground sewerage/sewage treatment | Urban areas | Centralized collection and treatment |

- **Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM)** — India's flagship sanitation program aiming at Open Defecation Free (ODF) status through construction of household toilets and behavior change; rural India was declared ODF in **2019** — verify current status/subsequent ODF-Plus phase details against latest program updates

## Solid Waste Management
- Segregation at source (wet/biodegradable vs dry/recyclable vs hazardous) is the foundational principle
- Methods of disposal: composting, sanitary landfill, incineration, recycling
- Municipal Solid Waste (Management and Handling) Rules govern urban solid waste — verify current version

## Air Pollution — Key Indicators
- **Air Quality Index (AQI)** — composite index based on major pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, CO, O3, etc.)
- **PM2.5** (particulate matter <2.5 micrometers) — most closely linked with cardiorespiratory morbidity/mortality due to deep alveolar penetration
- Indoor air pollution from biomass fuel/chulhas — major cause of respiratory disease in rural Indian women/children (addressed by clean cooking fuel schemes, e.g., Ujjwala Yojana providing LPG connections)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Slow sand filters work via the biological "schmutzdecke" layer; rapid sand filters require chemical coagulation and post-chlorination
- Ideal drinking water should show 0 coliforms per 100 mL
- Free residual chlorine of 0.2–0.5 mg/L after 30 minutes contact time ensures ongoing protection in distribution (verify current standard)
- Excess fluoride causes dental/skeletal fluorosis; deficiency is linked with dental caries
- Nitrate contamination of well water causes methemoglobinemia ("blue baby syndrome") in infants
- Swachh Bharat Mission is India's key sanitation program targeting Open Defecation Free status
- PM2.5 is the pollutant most strongly associated with cardiorespiratory health effects due to its ability to penetrate deep into alveoli
