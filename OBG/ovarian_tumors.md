# Ovarian Tumors

## Classification (WHO — by Cell of Origin)
| Category | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Epithelial tumors** (~65–70% of all ovarian tumors, ~90% of malignant) | Serous, mucinous, endometrioid, clear cell, Brenner tumor | Most common in postmenopausal women; malignant epithelial tumors are the most common ovarian cancers overall |
| **Germ cell tumors** | Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid), dysgerminoma, yolk sac tumor, immature teratoma, choriocarcinoma | Most common in **children/young women**; mature teratoma is the most common ovarian germ cell tumor overall (usually benign) |
| **Sex cord-stromal tumors** | Granulosa cell tumor, thecoma, fibroma, Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor | Often hormonally active (estrogen/androgen secreting) |
| **Metastatic tumors** | **Krukenberg tumor** (classically from GI — stomach — signet ring cells, bilateral) | Secondary to GI/breast primary |

## Benign vs Malignant Features (Suspicion on Imaging/Exam)
| Favors Benign | Favors Malignant |
|---|---|
| Unilateral, cystic, smooth, mobile | Bilateral, solid/complex, irregular, fixed |
| Simple thin-walled cyst on USG | Papillary projections, septations, ascites |
| Normal/low tumor markers | Elevated markers (age/menopause-status appropriate) |
| Slow growth | Rapid growth |
| Premenopausal, small (<5 cm) simple cyst | Postmenopausal with any solid/complex mass |

## Key Tumors & Associations
| Tumor | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst)** | Most common benign ovarian tumor in young women; contains elements of all 3 germ layers (hair, teeth, sebum); risk of torsion (most common complication); rare malignant transformation (usually squamous cell carcinoma) |
| **Serous cystadenoma/carcinoma** | Most common epithelial tumor type overall; serous carcinoma = most common malignant epithelial ovarian tumor; may show **psammoma bodies** on histology |
| **Mucinous cystadenoma** | Can become very large; rupture may cause **pseudomyxoma peritonei** |
| **Dysgerminoma** | Most common malignant germ cell tumor; analogous to testicular seminoma; radiosensitive; marker: **LDH** (also may have ↑hCG) |
| **Yolk sac (endodermal sinus) tumor** | Marker: **AFP**; Schiller-Duval bodies on histology |
| **Choriocarcinoma (ovarian)** | Marker: **β-hCG** |
| **Granulosa cell tumor** | Estrogen-secreting → precocious puberty (children), postmenopausal bleeding/endometrial hyperplasia (adults); **Call-Exner bodies** on histology; marker: **inhibin** |
| **Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor** | Androgen-secreting → virilization (hirsutism, deepening voice, clitoromegaly) |
| **Fibroma** | Associated with **Meigs syndrome** (ovarian fibroma + ascites + pleural effusion, resolves after tumor removal) |
| **Krukenberg tumor** | Bilateral, metastatic (classically from stomach — signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma), mucin-secreting |
| **Brenner tumor** | Transitional (urothelial-like) epithelium, usually benign, often incidental |

## Tumor Markers — Quick Reference
| Marker | Tumor Type |
|---|---|
| **CA-125** | Epithelial ovarian cancer (esp. serous) — also elevated in endometriosis, PID, pregnancy, other malignancies (nonspecific) |
| **AFP** | Yolk sac tumor, immature teratoma (component) |
| **β-hCG** | Choriocarcinoma, dysgerminoma (may be mildly elevated) |
| **LDH** | Dysgerminoma |
| **Inhibin, Estrogen** | Granulosa cell tumor |
| **Testosterone** | Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor |
| **CEA, CA 19-9** | Mucinous tumors |

## Risk Stratification Tools
- **Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI)** — combines menopausal status, USG features, CA-125 level
- **IOTA simple rules** — ultrasound-based benign vs malignant feature classification (per current knowledge — commonly tested tool)

## Clinical Features
- Often asymptomatic until large; vague abdominal discomfort, bloating, early satiety
- Pressure symptoms (urinary frequency, constipation)
- Menstrual irregularities (hormonally active tumors)
- Acute presentation: torsion (sudden severe pain), rupture, hemorrhage
- Advanced malignancy: ascites, abdominal distension, weight loss, cachexia

## Complications
- **Torsion** — most common with dermoid cysts (due to mobility/weight); surgical emergency
- Rupture, hemorrhage into cyst
- Malignant transformation (rare in most benign tumors)
- Infertility (rare, depends on tumor type/treatment)

## Management
| Scenario | Approach |
|---|---|
| Simple cyst, premenopausal, <5 cm | Observation, repeat USG (many resolve — functional cysts) |
| Persistent/enlarging/symptomatic benign-appearing mass | Cystectomy/oophorectomy (laparoscopic if feasible) |
| Suspicious for malignancy | Staging laparotomy — TAH + BSO + omentectomy + peritoneal washings + lymph node assessment; frozen section guides intraoperative decision |
| Confirmed malignancy | Surgical staging/debulking + adjuvant chemotherapy (platinum-based, e.g., carboplatin-paclitaxel) for epithelial cancers |
| Torsion | Emergency surgery — detorsion (if viable) or salpingo-oophorectomy |

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid) is the most common benign ovarian tumor in reproductive-age women and the most common tumor to undergo torsion
- Serous cystadenocarcinoma is the most common malignant epithelial ovarian tumor overall
- Dysgerminoma is the most common malignant germ cell tumor and is notably radiosensitive
- Granulosa cell tumor secretes estrogen, causing precocious puberty in children or postmenopausal bleeding in adults; Call-Exner bodies are characteristic
- Krukenberg tumor is a bilateral metastatic (classically gastric, signet-ring cell) ovarian tumor
- Meigs syndrome = ovarian fibroma + ascites + pleural effusion, resolving after tumor resection
- CA-125 is useful for monitoring treatment response in epithelial ovarian cancer but is not specific enough for population screening
