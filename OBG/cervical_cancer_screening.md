# Cervical Cancer & Screening

## Epidemiology & Etiology
- **Human papillomavirus (HPV)** infection is the necessary cause of nearly all cervical cancer — persistent high-risk HPV infection is the key etiological driver
- **High-risk oncogenic types: HPV 16 and 18** — account for the majority of cervical cancers (16 most associated with squamous cell carcinoma, 18 more associated with adenocarcinoma)
- Risk factors: early coitarche, multiple sexual partners, multiparity, smoking, immunosuppression (HIV), low socioeconomic status, lack of screening

## Pathogenesis
- Persistent high-risk HPV infection → integration into host genome → viral **E6 protein degrades p53**; **E7 protein inactivates Rb (retinoblastoma protein)** → loss of cell cycle control → progression through **cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN I → II → III)** → invasive carcinoma
- Transformation zone (squamocolumnar junction) is the most vulnerable site — where most dysplasia/cancer arises

## Histology
- **Squamous cell carcinoma** — most common histological type (~70–80%)
- **Adenocarcinoma** — second most common, arising from endocervical glandular epithelium; proportion has been rising

## Screening
| Test | Details |
|---|---|
| **Pap smear (cytology)** | Conventional or liquid-based; screening starts typically around age 21 (or per local guideline — some start at 25–30 in resource-limited settings); mainstay historically |
| **HPV DNA co-testing / primary HPV testing** | Increasingly recommended as primary screening tool (higher sensitivity than cytology alone) — per current guidelines, may be used alone or with cytology (co-testing) |
| **VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic acid)** | Low-cost, useful in resource-limited settings (India — "see-and-treat" approach); acetowhite areas suggest dysplasia |
| **Colposcopy** | Performed for abnormal screening results — directed biopsy of acetowhite/abnormal vascular areas |

*High-yield: Screening intervals and starting ages have been progressively extended and shifted toward HPV-based primary testing in updated international guidelines — classic teaching emphasized annual/frequent Pap smears; current guidance favors longer intervals (e.g., every 3–5 years depending on modality) once initial screens are normal. Verify against the latest guideline version referenced by your exam.*

## Bethesda System (Cytology Reporting) — Key Categories
- ASC-US (atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance)
- LSIL (low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion) — correlates with CIN I
- HSIL (high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion) — correlates with CIN II/III
- AGC (atypical glandular cells)

## Management of Precancerous Lesions
| Finding | Management |
|---|---|
| CIN I (LSIL) | Often observation/repeat testing — many regress spontaneously, especially in young women |
| CIN II/III (HSIL) | Excisional/ablative treatment — **LEEP (Loop Electrosurgical Excision Procedure)**, cold knife conization, or cryotherapy/ablation depending on setting and lesion extent |
| Persistent/recurrent high-grade lesion | Conization, occasionally hysterectomy if childbearing complete |

## Staging (FIGO) — Simplified
| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Confined to cervix |
| II | Beyond cervix but not to pelvic wall/lower third of vagina |
| III | Extends to pelvic wall/lower third of vagina/hydronephrosis |
| IV | Beyond true pelvis or involves bladder/rectal mucosa (IVA) or distant metastasis (IVB) |
- Cervical cancer is staged **clinically** (traditionally), though FIGO 2018 revision incorporates imaging and pathological findings — per current staging update

## Management by Stage
| Stage | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Early (IA1, microinvasive) | Conization/simple hysterectomy (fertility-sparing options in select IA1/IB1 cases — e.g., trachelectomy) |
| Early invasive (IB1/IIA, operable) | **Radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy (Wertheim's operation)** OR primary chemoradiation — comparable outcomes |
| Locally advanced (IIB–IVA) | **Concurrent chemoradiation** (cisplatin-based chemotherapy + external beam radiotherapy + brachytherapy) — treatment of choice |
| Metastatic/recurrent (IVB) | Palliative chemotherapy ± targeted therapy |

## HPV Vaccination
- Prophylactic vaccines (bivalent, quadrivalent, nonavalent depending on product) targeting high-risk types (16, 18) ± low-risk types (6, 11 — genital warts)
- Most effective when given **before sexual debut/HPV exposure** — typically recommended in early adolescence (per current national/WHO guidelines — age ranges vary; verify current schedule)
- Vaccination does not eliminate the need for continued screening (does not cover all oncogenic HPV types)

## Complications
- Ureteric obstruction/hydronephrosis (direct spread), fistula (vesicovaginal/rectovaginal) in advanced disease, most common cause of death = uremia from bilateral ureteric obstruction in untreated advanced disease

## High-Yield One-Liners
- HPV 16 and 18 are the most common high-risk types causing cervical cancer
- E6 (degrades p53) and E7 (inactivates Rb) are the key HPV oncoproteins driving carcinogenesis
- Squamous cell carcinoma is the most common histological subtype of cervical cancer
- Cervical cancer is classically staged clinically, unlike most other gynecological cancers which are staged surgically
- Radical hysterectomy and primary chemoradiation offer comparable outcomes for early-stage operable cervical cancer
- Concurrent cisplatin-based chemoradiation is standard for locally advanced disease
- HPV vaccination is most effective before sexual debut and does not replace the need for continued cervical screening
- VIA is a practical, low-cost screening alternative to Pap smear in low-resource settings like rural India
