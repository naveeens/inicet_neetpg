# Prenatal Diagnosis & Infections in Pregnancy

## Prenatal Diagnosis / Screening

### Aneuploidy Screening
| Test | Timing | Components |
|---|---|---|
| **Combined first-trimester screening** | 11–13+6 weeks | Nuchal translucency (NT) + maternal serum β-hCG + PAPP-A |
| **Double marker test** | 11–13+6 weeks | β-hCG + PAPP-A (serum-only component of combined test) |
| **Quadruple (Quad) marker test** | 15–20 weeks (second trimester) | β-hCG, AFP, unconjugated estriol, inhibin-A |
| **Triple marker test** | Second trimester | β-hCG, AFP, unconjugated estriol |
| **Cell-free fetal DNA (NIPT)** | From ~10 weeks | Highest sensitivity/specificity for common trisomies (21, 18, 13) among non-invasive tests — screening test, not diagnostic |

### Marker Patterns — Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21) vs Edwards (Trisomy 18) vs NTD
| Condition | AFP | β-hCG | Estriol | Inhibin-A |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Down syndrome (T21)** | ↓ | ↑ | ↓ | ↑ |
| **Edwards syndrome (T18)** | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | Normal/↓ |
| **Neural tube defect / open abdominal wall defect** | ↑↑ | Normal | Normal | Normal |

### Invasive Diagnostic Tests
| Test | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Chorionic villus sampling (CVS)** | 11–13 weeks | Earliest invasive test; slightly higher miscarriage/limb defect risk if done <10 weeks (avoid before 10 weeks) |
| **Amniocentesis** | 15–20 weeks (traditionally ~16 weeks) | Gold standard for karyotype; risk of miscarriage ~0.1–0.3% (per current knowledge) |
| **Cordocentesis (fetal blood sampling)** | >18 weeks | Used for rapid karyotype, fetal anemia assessment, in-utero transfusion access |

### Ultrasound Markers
- NT >3–3.5 mm (or above gestation-specific cutoff) — aneuploidy risk marker
- Soft markers for Down syndrome: nasal bone hypoplasia/absence, echogenic bowel, choroid plexus cyst (also T18), duodenal atresia ("double bubble"), cardiac defects (endocardial cushion defect/AV canal), single umbilical artery

## Infections in Pregnancy (TORCH & Others)

### TORCH Group — Overview
**T**oxoplasma, **O**ther (syphilis, varicella, parvovirus B19), **R**ubella, **C**ytomegalovirus, **H**erpes simplex

| Infection | Key Fetal/Neonatal Findings | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Toxoplasmosis** | Classic triad: chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications | Risk from cat feces, undercooked meat; treat with spiramycin (reduces transmission)/pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine regimens per protocol |
| **Rubella** | Classic triad: **cataract, deafness (sensorineural), cardiac defects (PDA most common)**; also microcephaly, "blueberry muffin" rash | **Highest risk of severe congenital rubella syndrome in first trimester** (especially <12 weeks); live vaccine — avoid in pregnancy, avoid conception for ~1 month after vaccination |
| **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)** | **Most common congenital infection**; periventricular calcifications, microcephaly, sensorineural hearing loss, hepatosplenomegaly, petechiae | Most infected infants asymptomatic at birth but may develop sequelae (hearing loss) later |
| **Herpes Simplex Virus** | Neonatal HSV mainly acquired **intrapartum** (contact with genital lesions) rather than transplacentally | Active genital lesions at term → **cesarean delivery** recommended to prevent neonatal transmission |
| **Syphilis** | Hydrops, hepatosplenomegaly, skin lesions; late: saddle nose, saber shins, Hutchinson's teeth, interstitial keratitis | Transplacental transmission possible at any gestation, risk ↑ with advancing gestation; treat with **penicillin** (safe and effective even in pregnancy) |
| **Varicella (Chickenpox)** | Congenital varicella syndrome (limb hypoplasia, skin scarring, chorioretinitis) if maternal infection in first/early second trimester; severe neonatal varicella if maternal infection close to delivery | VZIG for exposed susceptible pregnant women; avoid live vaccine in pregnancy |
| **Parvovirus B19** | Fetal anemia → **non-immune hydrops fetalis** (suppresses fetal erythropoiesis) | Monitor with serial MCA Doppler/USG for hydrops after exposure/infection |
| **Zika virus** | Microcephaly, other CNS anomalies | Relevant in endemic-exposure travel history |

### General Principles
- Primary maternal infection generally carries higher fetal risk than reactivation (true for CMV, toxoplasmosis)
- Earlier gestational infection → generally more severe structural anomalies; later infection → higher transmission rate but often less severe/more subclinical disease (rubella and CMV follow this general pattern; syphilis transmission risk increases with gestation)
- Screening in India: VDRL/syphilis serology and HIV testing are standard antenatal screens; routine universal TORCH panel screening is not uniformly recommended by all guidelines — often targeted based on clinical/USG findings (per current knowledge — verify local protocol)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Most common congenital infection overall = CMV; most common vaccine-preventable congenital infection = Rubella
- Rubella congenital triad: cataract, deafness, cardiac defect (PDA); highest risk in first trimester
- Toxoplasmosis classic triad: chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications
- HSV is transmitted to the neonate mainly intrapartum — active lesions at term warrant cesarean delivery
- Parvovirus B19 causes fetal anemia and non-immune hydrops via erythroid suppression
- Quadruple marker test pattern for Down syndrome: ↓AFP, ↑β-hCG, ↓estriol, ↑inhibin-A
- CVS is done earlier (11–13 weeks) than amniocentesis (15–20 weeks) but carries a slightly higher procedure-related risk if performed before 10 weeks
- Cell-free fetal DNA (NIPT) is a highly sensitive screening test, not a diagnostic test — abnormal results require confirmatory invasive testing
