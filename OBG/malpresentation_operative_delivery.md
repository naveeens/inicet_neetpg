# Malpresentation & Operative Delivery

## Definitions
- **Presentation**: the part of the fetus occupying the lower pole of the uterus (leading part)
- **Malpresentation**: any presentation other than vertex (occiput) — includes breech, face, brow, shoulder (transverse lie)
- **Malposition**: abnormal position of vertex presentation (e.g., occipitoposterior)

## Breech Presentation
### Types
| Type | Description | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Complete (flexed) breech | Hips and knees both flexed | Less common |
| **Frank breech** | Hips flexed, knees extended (legs extended up alongside trunk) | **Most common type** |
| Footling/incomplete breech | One or both feet/knees presenting below buttocks | Higher cord prolapse risk |

### Risk Factors
- Prematurity (breech more common before term — spontaneously converts to vertex in most by term), multiple pregnancy, polyhydramnios/oligohydramnios, uterine anomalies (e.g., bicornuate uterus), placenta previa, fetal anomalies (e.g., anencephaly, hydrocephalus)

### Management
- **External Cephalic Version (ECV)**: attempted at ~36–37 weeks in eligible candidates to convert to vertex; contraindicated in placenta previa, ruptured membranes, uterine anomaly, prior classical cesarean, non-reassuring fetal status
- Mode of delivery: **Cesarean section is generally preferred** for term breech per most current guidelines (based on landmark trial evidence showing better neonatal outcomes with planned cesarean) — vaginal breech delivery reserved for select cases with favorable criteria (adequate pelvis, flexed/frank breech, estimated weight in acceptable range, experienced operator) at some centers
- *High-yield: Classic teaching allowed vaginal breech delivery more liberally; a landmark multicenter trial shifted practice strongly toward planned cesarean for term breech — know the shift for "current guidelines vs classic teaching" framing.*

## Face Presentation
- Hyperextension of fetal head (denominator = **mentum**)
- **Mentoanterior** position: vaginal delivery possible (chin rotates anteriorly, face acts like an exaggerated vertex)
- **Mentoposterior** position: usually **cannot deliver vaginally** (persistent mentoposterior — head cannot flex/extend adequately) → cesarean section required
- Associated with anencephaly, large fetus, contracted pelvis

## Brow Presentation
- Attitude midway between full flexion and full extension — largest presenting diameter (**mentovertical**, ~13.5 cm)
- Usually **cannot deliver vaginally** unless converts to face or vertex during labor → cesarean section typically required

## Transverse Lie / Shoulder Presentation
- Fetal long axis perpendicular to maternal long axis
- Causes: grand multiparity (lax uterine/abdominal muscles), placenta previa, uterine anomalies, contracted pelvis, prematurity, polyhydramnios
- **Cannot deliver vaginally** (except very rare spontaneous evolution in a small preterm fetus) → **Cesarean section is mandatory**
- High risk of **cord prolapse** and **impacted/neglected shoulder presentation** leading to obstructed labor

## Occipitoposterior Position
- Vertex presentation but occiput points posteriorly (persistent OP)
- Associated with prolonged labor, deep transverse arrest
- Management: expectant (many rotate spontaneously), manual/instrumental rotation, or cesarean if arrest/failure to progress

## Operative Vaginal Delivery
### Forceps vs Vacuum (Ventouse)
| Feature | Forceps | Vacuum (Ventouse) |
|---|---|---|
| Application | Requires precise application over parietal bones/malar-mandibular area | Applied over flexion point on fetal scalp |
| Maternal trauma | Higher risk of perineal/vaginal lacerations | Lower maternal trauma |
| Fetal trauma | Facial nerve palsy, skull trauma if misapplied | **Cephalhematoma, subgaleal hemorrhage** more common |
| Cervical dilatation requirement | Full dilatation | Full dilatation |
| Position requirement | More position-flexible with rotational forceps (e.g., Kielland's) | Less effective for rotation |
| Gestational age limit | No strict lower limit | Avoid <34 weeks (fetal scalp fragility, risk of intracranial hemorrhage) |

### Prerequisites for Any Operative Vaginal Delivery ("ABCDEFGH" mnemonic concept)
- Full cervical dilatation, ruptured membranes, cephalic presentation with head engaged, known position, adequate pelvis, empty bladder, adequate analgesia, willingness to abandon and proceed to cesarean if unsuccessful

### Indications
- Prolonged second stage, fetal distress in second stage, maternal exhaustion, maternal conditions requiring avoidance of pushing (e.g., cardiac disease)

## Cesarean Section — Key Indications
- Absolute: contracted pelvis/cephalopelvic disproportion, placenta previa (major), transverse lie, previous classical cesarean
- Relative/common: fetal distress, failure to progress, malpresentation (breech per current practice, brow, mentoposterior face), previous cesarean (per case-by-case VBAC eligibility), multiple pregnancy with malpresentation of first twin

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Frank breech is the most common type of breech presentation
- Mentoanterior face presentation can deliver vaginally; mentoposterior generally cannot
- Brow presentation has the largest presenting diameter (mentovertical, ~13.5 cm) and usually requires cesarean unless it converts
- Transverse lie/shoulder presentation is an absolute indication for cesarean section (barring rare exceptions) due to inability to deliver vaginally
- Vacuum extraction carries higher risk of neonatal cephalhematoma/subgaleal hemorrhage; forceps carries higher maternal perineal trauma risk
- Avoid vacuum extraction before 34 weeks gestation due to fetal scalp/skull fragility
- Current practice favors planned cesarean for term breech presentation over vaginal breech delivery, a shift from older teaching
