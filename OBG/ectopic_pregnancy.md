# Ectopic Pregnancy

## Definition
Implantation of a fertilized ovum outside the endometrial cavity of the uterus.

## Sites
| Site | Frequency |
|---|---|
| **Fallopian tube — Ampulla** | Most common site overall (~70%) |
| Fallopian tube — Isthmus | Second most common tubal site; ruptures early (narrow lumen) |
| Fallopian tube — Fimbrial/infundibular | Less common |
| Interstitial (cornual) | Rare, ruptures later but catastrophically (rich vascularity, myometrial location) |
| Ovarian | Rare |
| **Cervical** | Rare, high hemorrhage risk |
| **Abdominal** | Rare |
| **Cesarean scar pregnancy** | Increasingly recognized, related to prior cesarean |
| Heterotopic pregnancy | Simultaneous intrauterine + ectopic pregnancy — risk increases with ART |

## Risk Factors
- **Prior ectopic pregnancy** (strongest risk factor for recurrence)
- **Prior pelvic inflammatory disease/tubal damage** (e.g., from Chlamydia/Gonorrhea infection)
- Tubal surgery (including sterilization/reversal), prior tubal ectopic
- Assisted reproductive technology (IVF), intrauterine device in situ (relative risk of ectopic among those who conceive with an IUD is increased, though overall pregnancy rate with IUD is very low)
- Smoking, increasing maternal age, endometriosis, congenital tubal anomalies

## Clinical Features
- **Classic triad**: amenorrhea (missed period), abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding/spotting
- Pain often unilateral adnexal; may become generalized with rupture
- Signs of hemoperitoneum if ruptured: dizziness, syncope, shoulder tip pain (diaphragmatic irritation from blood), signs of hypovolemic shock
- Examination: cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness/mass, **cul-de-sac fullness/tenderness (Douglas pouch)**
- Ruptured ectopic = surgical emergency — can present with hemodynamic collapse

## Diagnosis
| Test | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Urine/serum β-hCG** | Positive but often lower than expected for gestational age |
| **Serial serum β-hCG** | In normal early intrauterine pregnancy, β-hCG roughly doubles every 48 hours; suboptimal rise (or plateau) raises suspicion of ectopic or failing pregnancy |
| **Transvaginal ultrasound** | No intrauterine gestational sac despite β-hCG above the discriminatory zone (commonly cited ~1500–2000 mIU/mL, per current knowledge — verify assay-specific cutoff) suggests ectopic; may show adnexal mass, "tubal ring"/"donut sign," free fluid in pouch of Douglas |
| **Culdocentesis** | Historic test — non-clotting blood on aspiration from pouch of Douglas suggests hemoperitoneum; largely replaced by TVS |
| Diagnostic laparoscopy | Both diagnostic and therapeutic in equivocal/surgical cases |

## Management
### Hemodynamically Unstable / Ruptured
- Emergency resuscitation + **surgical management** (laparotomy or laparoscopy depending on stability) — **salpingectomy** (removal of tube) preferred if contralateral tube healthy/severe damage; **salpingostomy** (tube-conserving) an option if fertility preservation important and contralateral tube diseased

### Hemodynamically Stable — Options
| Option | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **Expectant management** | Very select cases — declining β-hCG, small/unruptured, minimal symptoms, close follow-up feasible |
| **Medical management — Methotrexate** | Hemodynamically stable, unruptured, no fetal cardiac activity, β-hCG below a defined threshold (commonly cited <5000 mIU/mL, per current knowledge — verify protocol), mass size within limits (commonly cited <3.5–4 cm), no contraindications (no significant liver/renal/hematologic disease, no breastfeeding); requires reliable follow-up with serial β-hCG monitoring |
| **Surgical (laparoscopic preferred if stable)** | Larger mass, higher β-hCG, fetal cardiac activity present, contraindication to methotrexate, or failed medical management |

### Methotrexate — Key Points
- Folate antagonist — inhibits trophoblastic proliferation
- Single-dose or multi-dose protocols exist
- Monitor β-hCG on days 4 and 7 post-treatment — expect ≥15% decline between day 4 and day 7; repeat dose or proceed to surgery if inadequate decline
- Avoid conception for a recommended interval after treatment (folate antagonist teratogenicity concern) and avoid NSAIDs/folic acid supplements during treatment (interfere with drug action/monitoring)

### Anti-D Prophylaxis
- Give anti-D immunoglobulin to Rh-negative women with ectopic pregnancy (sensitizing event), regardless of management method

## Complications
- Hemorrhagic shock (leading cause of first-trimester maternal mortality historically)
- Recurrent ectopic pregnancy
- Infertility (loss of tube/damage to remaining tube)
- Persistent trophoblastic tissue after conservative surgery (salpingostomy) — requires β-hCG follow-up

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Ampulla of the fallopian tube is the most common site of ectopic pregnancy
- Ectopic pregnancy is a leading cause of first-trimester maternal mortality due to hemorrhage
- Suboptimal rise in serial β-hCG (not doubling in 48 h) should raise suspicion for ectopic or non-viable pregnancy
- Transvaginal ultrasound has largely replaced culdocentesis for diagnosis
- Methotrexate is appropriate only in hemodynamically stable, unruptured cases without fetal cardiac activity and with β-hCG below the treatment threshold
- All Rh-negative women with ectopic pregnancy should receive anti-D immunoglobulin
- Prior ectopic pregnancy is the single strongest risk factor for a future ectopic pregnancy
