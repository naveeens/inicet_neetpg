# Otitis Media CSOM

## Relevant Anatomy
Eustachian tube connects middle ear to nasopharynx and equalizes middle ear pressure/drains secretions. In children it is **shorter, wider, and more horizontal**, and adenoid hypertrophy near its nasopharyngeal opening predisposes to reflux of infection and impaired drainage — hence children are far more prone to otitis media than adults. The middle ear communicates posteriorly with the mastoid antrum and air cell system; the attic (epitympanum) is the most common site of cholesteatoma formation.

## Acute Otitis Media (AOM)
### Etiology
- Preceded by upper respiratory tract infection → Eustachian tube dysfunction
- Organisms: **Streptococcus pneumoniae** (most common), *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*; viral (RSV, rhinovirus) also common, often as antecedent/co-infection
- Risk factors: young age, daycare attendance, bottle feeding (supine), cleft palate, Down syndrome, passive smoking

### Stages / Clinical Features
1. Stage of tubal occlusion — retracted dull TM
2. Stage of hyperemia (presuppurative) — severe earache, fever, congested TM
3. Stage of suppuration — bulging TM, severe pain, fever, hearing loss; may relieve pain if TM perforates
4. Stage of resolution/perforation — discharge (mucopurulent), pain relief after perforation
5. Stage of complications — if unresolved (mastoiditis, etc.)

- Classic otoscopy: bulging, erythematous, opacified tympanic membrane with loss of light reflex/landmarks

### Management
- Analgesics/antipyretics first-line for mild cases; many self-resolve (viral)
- Antibiotics: **Amoxicillin** first-line (high dose 80–90 mg/kg/day if risk factors); amoxicillin-clavulanate if treatment failure or recent antibiotic use
- **Myringotomy** if bulging TM with severe pain/toxicity not responding, or for complications
- Watchful waiting acceptable in select low-risk children >2 years with mild disease (per AAP guidelines)

## Otitis Media with Effusion (OME) / Serous Otitis Media
- Fluid in middle ear WITHOUT signs of acute infection; often post-AOM or from Eustachian tube dysfunction (adenoid hypertrophy, allergic rhinitis, barotrauma)
- Presents with hearing loss, sense of fullness; TM dull, retracted, may show air-fluid level/bubbles
- Tympanometry: **Type B (flat) curve**
- Management: watchful waiting (3 months, most resolve spontaneously); if persistent → **grommet/ventilation tube insertion** ± adenoidectomy

## Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM)
Defined as persistent middle ear discharge through a **permanent TM perforation** for >6 weeks (some texts: >3 months), with associated hearing loss.

### Two Types (classic exam classification)
| Feature | Tubotympanic (Safe type) | Atticoantral (Unsafe type) |
|---|---|---|
| Perforation | **Central** (pars tensa, margin of perforation surrounded by residual TM) | **Marginal/attic** (pars flaccida — Attic/Rinne perforation) |
| Discharge | Mucoid/mucopurulent, profuse, non-foul smelling | Scanty, foul-smelling |
| Hearing loss | Mild-moderate conductive | Can be severe; may have SNHL if labyrinth eroded |
| Cholesteatoma | Absent | **Present** (key feature) |
| Granulations/polyp | Uncommon | Common |
| Complications | Rare | **Common** — bone erosion, intracranial spread |
| Also called | "Safe" ear disease | "Unsafe"/dangerous ear disease |

### Cholesteatoma
- Keratinizing squamous epithelium in the middle ear/mastoid that behaves like an expanding, locally destructive mass (enzymatic bone erosion) — NOT a true neoplasm
- Classically arises in the **attic** via retraction pocket of pars flaccida
- Otoscopy: attic perforation with pearly-white flaky debris/keratin masses
- Can erode ossicles, lateral semicircular canal (→ vertigo, positive fistula test), facial nerve canal (→ facial palsy), tegmen tympani (→ intracranial spread)
- **Management is always surgical** — mastoidectomy (canal wall down or canal wall up depending on extent) — no role for conservative medical therapy alone

### Investigations
- Pure tone audiometry — assess type/degree of hearing loss
- **HRCT temporal bone** — best imaging to assess ossicular/bony erosion, extent of cholesteatoma, complications before surgery
- Culture and sensitivity of discharge for resistant/recurrent cases
- Tuning fork tests

### Management of CSOM
- **Tubotympanic**: aural toilet (suction clearance), topical/systemic antibiotics + ear drops (avoid ototoxic aminoglycoside drops if perforation present, though widely still used short-term), treat during dry/inactive phase; definitive surgery = **myringoplasty/tympanoplasty** once ear is dry and disease-free for a period
- **Atticoantral (with cholesteatoma)**: surgery is mandatory — **mastoidectomy** (with or without tympanoplasty/ossiculoplasty) to eradicate disease and prevent complications; medical therapy only temporizes before surgery

## Complications of Otitis Media / CSOM (High-Yield)
| Category | Complications |
|---|---|
| Extracranial | Mastoiditis, facial nerve palsy, labyrinthitis (→ vertigo, SNHL), petrositis (Gradenigo syndrome: otorrhea + retro-orbital pain + VI nerve palsy) |
| Intracranial | Meningitis (**most common intracranial complication**), extradural/subdural abscess, **sigmoid sinus thrombophlebitis**, otogenic brain abscess (temporal lobe or cerebellum — cerebellar abscess classically from atticoantral disease) |

- **Fistula test (positive)**: pressure on tragus/pneumatic otoscopy induces vertigo/nystagmus — indicates labyrinthine (semicircular canal) erosion by cholesteatoma
- Mastoiditis presents with post-auricular swelling, pushing pinna forward and downward-outward — needs urgent cortical mastoidectomy if not resolving with IV antibiotics

## High-Yield One-Liners
- AOM most common organism: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*
- Children more prone to AOM due to short, wide, horizontal Eustachian tube
- OME shows Type B (flat) tympanogram
- CSOM = discharge through permanent TM perforation >6 weeks
- Tubotympanic (safe) = central perforation, no cholesteatoma; Atticoantral (unsafe) = marginal/attic perforation, cholesteatoma present
- Cholesteatoma management is always surgical (mastoidectomy) — never purely medical
- HRCT temporal bone is the investigation of choice before ear surgery for CSOM
- Positive fistula test = labyrinthine erosion by cholesteatoma
- Most common intracranial complication of CSOM: meningitis
- Gradenigo syndrome = petrositis triad: otorrhea + retro-orbital pain + VI cranial nerve palsy
