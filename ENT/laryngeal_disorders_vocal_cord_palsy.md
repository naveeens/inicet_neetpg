# Laryngeal Disorders Vocal Cord Palsy

## Relevant Anatomy
- Larynx extends from epiglottis to lower border of cricoid cartilage (C3–C6); divided into **supraglottis, glottis (true vocal cords), subglottis**.
- Cartilages: thyroid, cricoid (only complete cartilaginous ring in airway — important in subglottic stenosis/pediatric airway obstruction), epiglottis, paired arytenoids.
- **Intrinsic muscles**: all supplied by **recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN)** EXCEPT **cricothyroid**, which is supplied by the **external laryngeal nerve** (branch of superior laryngeal nerve) — high-yield exception.
- **Posterior cricoarytenoid (PCA)** = only abductor of the vocal cords (opens the airway); all other intrinsic muscles are adductors — explains why bilateral RLN palsy causes airway obstruction (cords lie paramedian, adducted, unopposed).
- **Recurrent laryngeal nerve course**: right RLN loops under right subclavian artery; left RLN loops under the arch of aorta (longer course) — left RLN more frequently injured/involved in pathology (e.g., by aortic aneurysm, mediastinal disease, left-sided bronchogenic carcinoma — **Ortner's syndrome** with cardiomegaly).
- RLN is closely related to the **inferior thyroid artery** and thyroid gland — at high risk during **thyroidectomy** (most common cause of iatrogenic RLN palsy).
- **Superior laryngeal nerve**: internal branch = sensory to larynx above cords; external branch = motor to cricothyroid (tenses vocal cords, raises pitch) — injury (e.g., during thyroidectomy, ligating superior thyroid vessels) causes voice fatigue/loss of high pitch, not hoarseness/aphonia.

## Vocal Cord Palsy
### Etiology (Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Palsy)
| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| Iatrogenic (**most common overall**) | Thyroidectomy, cardiac/thoracic/esophageal surgery, carotid endarterectomy |
| Neoplastic | Bronchogenic carcinoma (esp. left, at aortopulmonary window), esophageal carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, mediastinal lymphadenopathy |
| Cardiovascular | Aortic aneurysm (left RLN), Ortner's (cardiovocal) syndrome — cardiomegaly compressing left RLN |
| Neurological | Brainstem lesions (CN X nucleus — e.g., lateral medullary/Wallenberg syndrome), skull base tumors |
| Idiopathic/viral | Often unilateral, may recover |

### Positions of the Vocal Cord (Classic Teaching)
- **Median**: complete adduction (seen in normal phonation and in compensated unilateral palsy)
- **Paramedian**: slightly lateral to median — most common position in unilateral RLN palsy (some tone from cricothyroid preserved as it is spared)
- **Intermediate/cadaveric**: fully abducted, seen when both SLN and RLN are paralyzed (complete vagal palsy)

### Clinical Features
- **Unilateral RLN palsy**: hoarseness of voice (breathy voice); may compensate over time as the contralateral (healthy) cord crosses midline to meet the paralyzed cord during phonation — voice may improve
- **Bilateral RLN palsy**: cords lie paramedian (adducted) → **airway obstruction/stridor** is the dominant problem; voice may be relatively preserved (deceptive) — this is an emergency requiring tracheostomy in many cases
- **Combined SLN + RLN (complete vagal palsy)**: cord in cadaveric position — severe hoarseness/aphonia + aspiration risk (loss of sensory protection)

### Investigations
- **Indirect/fibreoptic laryngoscopy or videolaryngostroboscopy** — direct visualization of cord mobility/position
- CT neck/chest (from skull base to aortic arch, "nerve monitoring" pathway) — to find the cause when palsy is idiopathic clinically, since RLN course is long
- EMG (laryngeal electromyography) — assess reinnervation potential/prognosis

### Management
- Treat underlying cause if identifiable and treatable
- Unilateral: voice therapy first; if persistent disabling dysphonia — **medialization thyroplasty (Type I)** or injection laryngoplasty (e.g., with fillers) to bring paralyzed cord to midline for better glottic closure
- Bilateral (airway-threatening): **tracheostomy** may be needed emergently; definitive options include cordotomy/arytenoidectomy (posterior) or lateralization procedures to widen airway (trade-off: airway improvement vs. voice quality)

## Laryngitis
- **Acute laryngitis**: viral URIs most common cause; also vocal abuse, irritants (smoking); presents with hoarseness, sore throat, cough; management supportive — voice rest, steam inhalation, avoid irritants; usually self-limiting
- **Chronic laryngitis**: due to chronic vocal abuse, smoking, GERD (laryngopharyngeal reflux — very high-yield modern cause), chronic sinusitis with postnasal drip; presents with persistent hoarseness — needs laryngoscopy to exclude malignancy if smoker/prolonged symptoms
- **Reinke's edema**: chronic edema of superficial lamina propria of vocal cords, strongly associated with **smoking**; causes deep, husky voice — classic in smokers

## Vocal Cord Nodules vs Polyps vs Contact Ulcers
| Lesion | Cause | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Vocal nodules ("singer's nodules") | Chronic vocal abuse/misuse | **Bilateral**, at junction of anterior 1/3–posterior 2/3 of cord (point of maximal vibration); common in singers, teachers, children |
| Vocal polyp | Single episode of vocal trauma/abuse or chronic irritation | Usually **unilateral**, pedunculated or sessile |
| Contact ulcer/granuloma | Vocal abuse (forceful glottal closure) or GERD, intubation trauma | Posterior third of cords (over vocal process of arytenoid) |

- Management: voice rest + speech therapy first-line for nodules (often regress); surgical excision (microlaryngeal surgery) if persistent/polyps

## Laryngeal Carcinoma
- Strongly associated with **smoking and alcohol** (synergistic risk); most common histology = **squamous cell carcinoma**
- Classified by site: **glottic** (best prognosis — early hoarseness due to cord involvement, limited lymphatic drainage from true cords so nodal spread is late), **supraglottic** (rich lymphatics — often presents late with neck node metastasis, worse prognosis), **subglottic** (rare, often presents late)
- Clinical features: progressive hoarseness (glottic — earliest symptom), throat pain, referred otalgia, dysphagia, hemoptysis, stridor/airway obstruction (late), neck mass (nodal spread — more common with supraglottic)
- Investigations: **direct laryngoscopy with biopsy** (gold standard for diagnosis), CT/MRI neck for staging (cartilage invasion, extent), chest imaging for metastasis
- Management: depends on stage — early glottic disease may be treated with **radiotherapy or transoral laser microsurgery/partial laryngectomy** (organ preservation, excellent cure rates); advanced disease may need **total laryngectomy** with neck dissection ± chemoradiation; total laryngectomy patients require permanent tracheostome and voice rehabilitation (esophageal speech, tracheoesophageal puncture with valve, or electrolarynx)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- All intrinsic laryngeal muscles are supplied by RLN except cricothyroid (external laryngeal nerve)
- Posterior cricoarytenoid = sole abductor of vocal cords; its bilateral paralysis (adductors unopposed) causes airway obstruction
- Left RLN has a longer intrathoracic course (loops under aortic arch) — more often involved by mediastinal/cardiac/left lung pathology
- Most common cause of RLN palsy overall: thyroid surgery (iatrogenic)
- Unilateral RLN palsy → hoarseness; bilateral RLN palsy → stridor/airway obstruction (voice may be preserved)
- External laryngeal nerve injury → voice fatigue/loss of high pitch, not hoarseness
- Reinke's edema is classically associated with smoking
- Vocal nodules are bilateral and due to vocal abuse; vocal polyps are usually unilateral
- Glottic carcinoma presents earliest (hoarseness) and has the best prognosis due to sparse lymphatics of true vocal cords
- Direct laryngoscopy with biopsy is the definitive diagnostic test for laryngeal carcinoma
