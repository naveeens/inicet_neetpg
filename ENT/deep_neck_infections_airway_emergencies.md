# Deep Neck Infections Airway Emergencies

## Relevant Anatomy
The neck is organized into fascial planes/potential spaces defined by layers of the deep cervical fascia (superficial, pretracheal/visceral, prevertebral, carotid sheath). Infections spread along these planes:
- **Peritonsillar space**: between tonsillar capsule and superior constrictor muscle — site of quinsy (peritonsillar abscess), a complication of acute tonsillitis.
- **Submandibular space** (subdivided into sublingual and submylohyoid/submaxillary spaces by mylohyoid muscle) — site of **Ludwig's angina**, usually of dental origin (2nd/3rd molar infection).
- **Parapharyngeal space**: lateral to pharynx, extends from skull base to hyoid; contains carotid sheath — infection here risks great vessel involvement (carotid artery erosion, internal jugular vein thrombosis).
- **Retropharyngeal space**: between buccopharyngeal fascia and prevertebral fascia, extends from skull base to mediastinum (T1-T2) — infection can track down into the **mediastinum** (descending necrotizing mediastinitis), a feared complication.
- **Danger space**: between alar and prevertebral fascia, extends to the diaphragm — even faster spread to mediastinum than retropharyngeal space.
- Retropharyngeal lymph nodes are prominent in children (regress by ~age 4–5) — explains why retropharyngeal abscess is predominantly a **pediatric** disease (usually secondary to nasopharyngitis/URI), whereas in adults it is more often due to trauma/foreign body/instrumentation.

## Peritonsillar Abscess (Quinsy)
- Most common deep neck space infection; complication of acute bacterial tonsillitis — pus collects between tonsil capsule and superior constrictor
- Polymicrobial (aerobic + anaerobic, including *Streptococcus*, anaerobes)
- Clinical features: severe unilateral throat pain, **trismus**, "hot potato"/muffled voice, drooling, uvula pushed to the contralateral side, ipsilateral soft palate swelling, bulging of the anterior tonsillar pillar
- Management: **needle aspiration or incision & drainage** + antibiotics (IV then oral); tonsillectomy considered if recurrent quinsy or "interval/quinsy tonsillectomy" in select cases
- Differentiate from peritonsillar cellulitis (no drainable pus, treated with antibiotics alone)

## Ludwig's Angina
- Rapidly progressive **bilateral** cellulitis (not a discrete abscess initially) of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces — classically from an **infected lower 2nd/3rd molar tooth**
- Polymicrobial (mixed aerobic-anaerobic oral flora)
- Clinical features: brawny, board-like induration of the floor of mouth and submandibular region ("bull neck"), tongue pushed up and back (**elevated, protruding tongue**) → progressive airway obstruction, drooling, dysphagia, trismus, fever
- **Airway management is the top priority** — this is a true emergency; may require awake fiberoptic intubation or **tracheostomy** (avoid blind nasal/oral intubation attempts that can worsen obstruction; avoid deep sedation/paralysis before securing airway)
- Treatment: high-dose IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (covering anaerobes) + surgical decompression (drainage of submandibular space) + dental source control ± extraction
- Complication if untreated: descending mediastinitis, airway obstruction/asphyxia (major cause of death)

## Retropharyngeal Abscess
- Predominantly a disease of **young children** (<6 years) — from suppuration of retropharyngeal lymph nodes secondary to nasopharyngitis, otitis media, or adenoiditis; in adults usually from trauma/foreign body/endoscopic instrumentation/vertebral osteomyelitis
- Clinical features: fever, refusal to feed, neck stiffness/torticollis, drooling, muffled voice, respiratory distress, **bulge in posterior pharyngeal wall**
- Investigation: **lateral neck X-ray** — widened prevertebral soft tissue shadow (>7mm at C2 or >14mm at C6 in children is a classic threshold cited, though absolute cutoffs vary by source and age) is a bedside screening clue; CT neck with contrast confirms and delineates abscess vs. cellulitis
- Management: airway monitoring, IV antibiotics, **surgical incision and drainage** (transoral for localized abscess or external approach) if frank abscess; risk of aspiration if it ruptures spontaneously, and risk of extension into danger space/mediastinum

## Parapharyngeal Abscess
- Often secondary to tonsillitis, dental infection, or extension from peritonsillar/parotid infection
- Clinical features: trismus, swelling below angle of mandible, medial bulge of lateral pharyngeal wall, torticollis; can involve carotid sheath → risk of **internal jugular vein thrombosis (Lemierre's syndrome-type picture)** and carotid artery erosion/pseudoaneurysm — vascular complications are the most feared
- Management: IV antibiotics + surgical drainage (external cervical approach usually preferred over transoral, given proximity to great vessels)

## Acute Epiglottitis (Supraglottitis)
- Classically caused by ***Haemophilus influenzae* type b** (historically, in unvaccinated children) — incidence has dropped markedly with Hib vaccination; now seen more in adults and can be due to other organisms (*S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *S. pyogenes*)
- Rapid onset: high fever, severe sore throat/odynophagia, muffled "hot potato" voice, **drooling**, **inspiratory stridor**, patient prefers **tripod/sniffing position** leaning forward
- **Avoid throat examination/tongue depressor and avoid distressing the child (e.g., IV lines, lying supine) until airway is secured** — can precipitate complete airway obstruction
- Imaging (only if patient is stable enough): **lateral neck X-ray shows "thumb sign"** (swollen, rounded epiglottis) — contrast with the "steeple sign" of croup (subglottic narrowing)
- Definitive diagnosis: direct visualization (ideally in OR/controlled setting with anesthesia team ready) shows cherry-red, swollen epiglottis
- Management: secure airway first (elective intubation/tracheostomy in a controlled setting is safer than waiting for crisis), IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone/cefotaxime), steroids sometimes used adjunctively
- This is a true emergency — do NOT attempt to examine the throat with a tongue depressor in a child with suspected epiglottitis in the ER without airway backup

## Croup (Acute Laryngotracheobronchitis) — Contrast with Epiglottitis
| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 6 months–3 years | Any age (classically 2–6 y pre-vaccine era) |
| Onset | Gradual, preceded by URI | Rapid (hours) |
| Organism | Parainfluenza virus (viral) | *H. influenzae* type b (bacterial), other bacteria now |
| Cough | Barking/seal-like cough present | Usually absent |
| Voice | Hoarse | Muffled |
| X-ray sign | **Steeple sign** (subglottic narrowing) | **Thumb sign** (swollen epiglottis) |
| Position | Comfortable lying down usually | Sits up, leans forward, drooling |
| Management | Usually supportive: humidified O2, nebulized epinephrine, dexamethasone; rarely needs intubation | Often needs airway securing (intubation/tracheostomy) |

## Foreign Body Airway Obstruction
- Common in children (peanuts, small objects) and can be seen in adults (meat bolus, dentures)
- Complete obstruction: sudden inability to speak/cough/breathe, cyanosis — **Heimlich maneuver (abdominal thrusts)** in adults/older children; back blows + chest thrusts in infants (<1 year, to avoid abdominal organ injury)
- If obstruction complete and maneuvers fail → **emergency cricothyroidotomy** (rapid access) is preferred over tracheostomy in the acute crash airway setting, given speed and lower complication rate in emergency hands; tracheostomy is preferred as a planned/definitive procedure
- Radiolucent foreign bodies may need bronchoscopy for diagnosis if suspicion is high despite normal X-ray

## Tracheostomy — Key Points
- Indications: prolonged mechanical ventilation, upper airway obstruction (tumors, bilateral vocal cord palsy, deep neck infections with impending obstruction, facial trauma), pulmonary toilet in patients with poor cough reflex
- Landmark: between 2nd and 3rd (or 3rd and 4th) tracheal rings; avoid 1st ring (risk of subglottic stenosis) and going too low (risk of tracheo-innominate artery fistula)
- Complications: early (hemorrhage, pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema, tube displacement) vs. late (tracheal stenosis, tracheoesophageal fistula, tracheo-innominate artery fistula — catastrophic bleed, granulation tissue)
- **Cricothyroidotomy**: faster, simpler, preferred in emergency/crash airway situations; converted to formal tracheostomy within 24–48 hours if prolonged airway support needed (to reduce risk of subglottic stenosis with prolonged cricothyroidotomy)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Ludwig's angina is classically odontogenic (lower 2nd/3rd molar) and causes bilateral submandibular space cellulitis with tongue pushed up/back — airway control is the priority
- Peritonsillar abscess pushes the uvula to the opposite side and causes trismus; managed by needle aspiration/I&D
- Retropharyngeal abscess is a disease of young children, presenting with neck stiffness and posterior pharyngeal wall bulge; danger is descending mediastinitis
- Lateral neck X-ray: "thumb sign" = epiglottitis; "steeple sign" = croup
- Never examine the throat with a tongue depressor in suspected acute epiglottitis without airway backup available
- Epiglottitis classically caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b (incidence reduced by Hib vaccine)
- Parapharyngeal space infection risks carotid sheath involvement — internal jugular vein thrombosis and carotid erosion are feared vascular complications
- Emergency crash airway: cricothyroidotomy preferred over tracheostomy for speed
- Danger space communicates directly down to the diaphragm — fastest route for neck infection to reach the mediastinum
