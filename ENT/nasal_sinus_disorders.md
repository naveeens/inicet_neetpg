# Nasal Sinus Disorders

## Relevant Anatomy
- **Nasal septum**: cartilaginous (quadrilateral cartilage) anteriorly, bony (perpendicular plate of ethmoid superiorly, vomer inferoposteriorly) posteriorly.
- **Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus)**: anteroinferior part of septum — anastomosis of sphenopalatine, greater palatine, superior labial (from facial artery), and anterior ethmoidal arteries. **Most common site of epistaxis (especially in children/young adults).**
- **Paranasal sinuses**: maxillary (largest, floor near molar tooth roots — dental infection can cause sinusitis), frontal, ethmoid (anterior + posterior), sphenoid.
- **Ostiomeatal complex**: common drainage pathway for maxillary, frontal, and anterior ethmoid sinuses into the **middle meatus** — key target of Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS); obstruction here is central to chronic rhinosinusitis pathogenesis.
- **Sphenopalatine artery** (branch of maxillary artery): major source of severe **posterior epistaxis**.
- Cribriform plate (roof of nose, part of ethmoid) — thin bone; fracture here → CSF rhinorrhea, anosmia risk.

## Allergic Rhinitis
- Type I hypersensitivity (IgE-mediated) to inhaled allergens (dust mite, pollen)
- Clinical features: paroxysmal sneezing, watery rhinorrhea, nasal itching, nasal obstruction; "allergic salute," "allergic shiners" (infraorbital darkening), Dennie-Morgan lines
- Examination: pale, bluish, boggy inferior turbinates/nasal mucosa (contrasts with red/inflamed mucosa of infective rhinitis)
- Investigations: skin prick test, serum IgE, nasal smear/blood eosinophilia
- Management: allergen avoidance, **intranasal corticosteroids** (most effective single agent), oral/intranasal antihistamines (non-sedating preferred), leukotriene antagonists, immunotherapy for refractory/specific allergen-driven disease

## Chronic Rhinosinusitis (CRS) and Nasal Polyps
- CRS = sinonasal inflammation >12 weeks; typically due to ostiomeatal complex obstruction → impaired mucociliary clearance and secondary infection
- CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) vs without (CRSsNP)
- **Nasal polyps**: pale, grey, glistening, insensitive (non-tender), mobile masses, usually arise from ethmoid sinus/middle meatus; bilateral polyps in a child should raise suspicion for **cystic fibrosis**
- **Antrochoanal polyp**: arises from maxillary antrum, unilateral, extends through the natural ostium/accessory ostium into the choana/nasopharynx — classically in children/young adults; managed surgically (endoscopic removal with attention to antral part to prevent recurrence)
- Investigations: diagnostic nasal endoscopy, **CT paranasal sinuses (coronal, "screening" view)** — gold standard for extent of disease before FESS
- Management: intranasal/systemic corticosteroids, saline irrigation, antibiotics for acute-on-chronic bacterial exacerbation; **FESS** for medically refractory disease or complications

## Acute Sinusitis
- Usually follows viral URI; bacterial superinfection (*S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis*) suspected if symptoms persist >10 days or worsen after initial improvement ("double sickening")
- Maxillary sinusitis most common (largest sinus, dependent ostium); presents with facial pain/pressure, purulent nasal discharge, postnasal drip, fever
- Frontal sinusitis — forehead pain/tenderness, risk of complications (Pott's puffy tumor — subperiosteal abscess from frontal osteomyelitis)
- Management: analgesics, decongestants, nasal saline; antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate) if bacterial features/persistence

## Epistaxis
| Type | Site | Typical Population | Vessel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anterior (most common, ~90%) | **Little's area** (Kiesselbach's plexus) | Children, young adults | Anastomosis of ethmoidal, sphenopalatine, facial artery branches |
| Posterior | Posterolateral wall / near sphenopalatine foramen | Elderly, hypertensives, atherosclerosis | **Sphenopalatine artery** (branch of internal maxillary artery) |

### Management Ladder
1. First aid: sit up, lean forward, firm pressure on cartilaginous (lower soft) part of nose for 10–15 minutes
2. Identify and cauterize bleeding point (silver nitrate/electrocautery) if visualized — anterior bleeds
3. Anterior nasal packing (ribbon gauze/nasal tampon) if cautery fails or site not seen
4. Posterior epistaxis: **posterior nasal pack** (Foley catheter/Brighton balloon or traditional postnasal pack)
5. Refractory bleeding: **endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation/cautery** (increasingly preferred) or internal maxillary/ethmoidal artery ligation; angiographic embolization in select high-risk/recurrent cases
6. Always check BP and coagulation profile — epistaxis can be a presenting sign of hypertension or bleeding disorders (also consider juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma in adolescent males with recurrent severe epistaxis)

## Deviated Nasal Septum (DNS)
- Common cause of unilateral nasal obstruction; may be traumatic or developmental
- Associated with recurrent sinusitis (obstructs ostiomeatal complex), headache, epistaxis (from exposed septal spur)
- Management: **septoplasty** for symptomatic DNS (preserves more mucosa/structure than older submucous resection)

## CSF Rhinorrhea
- Leak of CSF into nose via defect in skull base (cribriform plate, ethmoid roof, sphenoid) — traumatic (most common, e.g., anterior skull base fracture) or spontaneous/iatrogenic (post-FESS)
- Confirm with **beta-2 transferrin** (highly specific for CSF) in nasal discharge; also check glucose (CSF glucose similar to blood, unlike normal nasal mucus which is low)
- Risk of ascending meningitis — avoid nasal packing/blowing nose; management often conservative initially (bed rest, head elevation), surgical repair (endoscopic) if persistent

## Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma (JNA)
- Benign but locally aggressive vascular tumor; classically **adolescent males**
- Presents with recurrent, profuse, unprovoked epistaxis + progressive nasal obstruction
- **Holman-Miller sign**: anterior bowing of posterior wall of maxillary sinus on imaging (CT)
- Biopsy is **contraindicated** (risk of severe hemorrhage) — diagnosis by imaging (CT/MRI) ± angiography
- Management: preoperative embolization followed by surgical excision

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus) = most common site of epistaxis, especially in children
- Posterior epistaxis in elderly/hypertensive patients → sphenopalatine artery, needs posterior packing
- Nasal polyps are typically pale and insensitive to touch (unlike hypertrophied turbinate, which is tender/sensitive) — classic differentiating clinical sign
- Bilateral nasal polyps in a child should prompt workup for cystic fibrosis
- Antrochoanal polyp: unilateral, arises from maxillary antrum, extends into nasopharynx, seen in children/young adults
- CT paranasal sinuses (coronal) is the imaging of choice before FESS
- Beta-2 transferrin confirms CSF rhinorrhea
- Never biopsy a suspected juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma — risk of catastrophic bleeding
- Holman-Miller sign (antral posterior wall bowing) is classic for JNA
