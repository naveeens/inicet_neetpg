# GI Bleeding Peptic Ulcer Disease

## Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

### Etiology
| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Helicobacter pylori | Most common cause overall (both gastric and duodenal ulcers) |
| NSAIDs | Second most common cause; inhibit protective prostaglandins (COX-1) |
| Zollinger-Ellison syndrome | Gastrin-secreting tumor — rare but classic cause of refractory/multiple ulcers |
| Stress ulcers | Critically ill patients (Cushing's ulcer — with ↑ICP; Curling's ulcer — with burns) |

### Gastric vs Duodenal Ulcer
| Feature | Gastric Ulcer | Duodenal Ulcer |
|---|---|---|
| Pain timing | Worsens with food (weight loss) | Relieved by food, recurs 2–3h later, classic nocturnal pain |
| Age | Older | Younger |
| Acid secretion | Normal/low | High |
| Malignancy risk | Present (biopsy edges of gastric ulcers to exclude malignancy) | Essentially none |

### Diagnosis
- Upper GI endoscopy: gold standard, allows direct visualization + biopsy (gastric ulcers biopsied to exclude malignancy; duodenal ulcers rarely require biopsy)
- H. pylori testing: urea breath test, stool antigen (both good for initial diagnosis and confirming eradication), rapid urease test/biopsy at endoscopy, serology (indicates exposure, not necessarily active infection — least useful for confirming cure)

### Management
- H. pylori eradication: triple therapy (PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin) or quadrant/bismuth-based quadruple therapy depending on local resistance patterns — clarithromycin resistance increasingly limits standard triple therapy in many regions, verify against local resistance data
- NSAID-induced: stop NSAID if possible, PPI therapy; if NSAID must continue, co-prescribe PPI or use COX-2 selective agent
- PPIs are mainstay of acid suppression/ulcer healing regardless of cause

## Upper GI Bleeding

### Etiology
- Peptic ulcer disease (most common cause of upper GI bleed overall)
- Esophageal/gastric varices (portal hypertension — high mortality per episode)
- Mallory-Weiss tear (mucosal tear at gastroesophageal junction, classically post-forceful vomiting/retching)
- Erosive gastritis/esophagitis, malignancy, Dieulafoy lesion, aortoenteric fistula (rare, catastrophic — think in patients with prior aortic graft)

### Clinical Features
- Hematemesis (fresh blood or "coffee-ground"), melena (black tarry stool — suggests upper source or slow transit), hematochezia if brisk upper bleed with rapid transit
- Signs of hemodynamic instability: tachycardia, hypotension, orthostatic changes

### Management
1. Resuscitation first: IV access (2 large-bore lines), crystalloids, blood transfusion as needed (restrictive transfusion strategy, target Hb ~7 g/dL in most patients without active cardiovascular disease — landmark trial evidence supports restrictive over liberal transfusion)
2. Risk stratification: Glasgow-Blatchford score (pre-endoscopy risk assessment), Rockall score (post-endoscopy prognosis)
3. IV PPI (started empirically, even before endoscopy, in suspected peptic ulcer bleed — reduces high-risk stigmata)
4. If variceal bleed suspected: add octreotide/terlipressin + prophylactic antibiotics (as per cirrhosis/portal hypertension management)
5. Upper GI endoscopy within 24 hours (urgent if unstable) — diagnostic and therapeutic (clipping, thermal coagulation, injection therapy, band ligation for varices)
6. Interventional radiology (angioembolization) or surgery if endoscopic therapy fails

### Forrest Classification (Ulcer Bleeding Risk on Endoscopy)
| Class | Finding | Rebleed risk |
|---|---|---|
| Ia/Ib | Active spurting/oozing bleed | High |
| IIa | Non-bleeding visible vessel | High |
| IIb | Adherent clot | Intermediate |
| IIc | Flat pigmented spot | Low |
| III | Clean base | Very low |

## Lower GI Bleeding

### Etiology
- Diverticulosis (most common cause of acute lower GI bleed in older adults)
- Hemorrhoids (most common cause of minor/intermittent rectal bleeding overall)
- Colorectal malignancy/polyps, angiodysplasia (especially right colon, associated with aortic stenosis — Heyde syndrome), IBD, ischemic colitis, infectious colitis

### Clinical Features
- Hematochezia (fresh/altered blood per rectum) most common presentation; massive upper GI bleed can also present as hematochezia if very brisk

### Management
- Resuscitation as above
- Colonoscopy after bowel preparation (once stabilized) — diagnostic and therapeutic
- CT angiography or radionuclide scanning if bleeding is brisk/localization unclear
- Angioembolization or surgery for refractory/massive bleeding

## High-Yield One-Liners
- H. pylori and NSAIDs are the two leading causes of peptic ulcer disease
- Gastric ulcer pain worsens with food; duodenal ulcer pain classically improves with food and recurs a few hours later
- Always biopsy gastric ulcer edges to rule out malignancy; duodenal ulcers are essentially never malignant
- Melena suggests an upper GI (or slow-transit right colonic) bleeding source; hematochezia suggests lower GI or a brisk upper GI bleed
- A restrictive transfusion strategy (target Hb ~7 g/dL) is preferred over liberal transfusion in most non-cardiac GI bleed patients
- Empirical IV PPI should be started before endoscopy in suspected upper GI bleed
- Diverticulosis is the most common cause of acute lower GI bleeding in older adults; angiodysplasia is classically associated with aortic stenosis (Heyde syndrome)
- Mallory-Weiss tear classically follows forceful retching/vomiting and is usually self-limiting
