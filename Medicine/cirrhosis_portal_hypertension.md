# Cirrhosis Portal Hypertension

## Cirrhosis

### Definition/Etiology
End-stage of chronic liver injury — diffuse fibrosis with regenerative nodules, disrupting normal architecture and vasculature.

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Alcohol-related liver disease | Common cause globally |
| Chronic viral hepatitis (B, C) | Major cause, especially in endemic regions |
| Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD/NASH) | Rising incidence, linked to metabolic syndrome/obesity — increasingly common cause worldwide |
| Autoimmune (autoimmune hepatitis, PBC, PSC) | Less common, specific antibody patterns |
| Wilson's disease, hemochromatosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency | Genetic/metabolic causes, consider in younger patients |

### Clinical Features
- Compensated: often asymptomatic, may have fatigue, mild derangement in labs
- Decompensated: jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy
- Stigmata of chronic liver disease: spider angiomata, palmar erythema, gynecomastia, testicular atrophy, caput medusae, Dupuytren's contracture, asterixis, fetor hepaticus, leukonychia

### Investigations
- LFTs (may be normal in advanced cirrhosis — "burnt out"), albumin (low), PT/INR (prolonged — synthetic dysfunction)
- Imaging: USG (nodular liver, splenomegaly), elastography (Fibroscan) for non-invasive fibrosis staging
- Liver biopsy: gold standard for staging but often unnecessary if clinical/imaging/lab picture is clear
- Severity scoring: Child-Pugh score (bilirubin, albumin, INR, ascites, encephalopathy) and MELD score (bilirubin, creatinine, INR, sodium) — MELD used for transplant prioritization

### Child-Pugh Classification
| Class | Points | 1-year survival (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 5–6 | Best |
| B | 7–9 | Intermediate |
| C | 10–15 | Worst |

## Portal Hypertension

### Pathophysiology
Increased resistance to portal flow (cirrhosis = most common cause, intrahepatic) → portal pressure rise → collateral formation (varices) + splenomegaly + ascites.

### Classification by Site
| Site | Examples |
|---|---|
| Pre-hepatic | Portal vein thrombosis, splenic vein thrombosis |
| Intrahepatic | Cirrhosis (most common overall), schistosomiasis (presinusoidal) |
| Post-hepatic | Budd-Chiari syndrome, right heart failure, constrictive pericarditis |

### Clinical Manifestations
- Esophageal/gastric varices (risk of life-threatening bleed)
- Splenomegaly (hypersplenism → thrombocytopenia)
- Ascites, caput medusae, hemorrhoids

## Esophageal Varices
- Screening: upper GI endoscopy at diagnosis of cirrhosis and periodically thereafter
- Primary prophylaxis (no bleed yet, varices present): non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol/nadolol) or endoscopic band ligation for high-risk varices
- Acute variceal bleed management:
  1. Resuscitation (IV fluids, blood products as needed — avoid over-transfusion, target Hb ~7-8 g/dL to avoid rebound portal pressure increase)
  2. Vasoactive drugs: octreotide/terlipressin (splanchnic vasoconstriction) started early
  3. Prophylactic antibiotics (reduces mortality, prevents SBP/infection — ceftriaxone commonly used)
  4. Endoscopy within 12 hours: band ligation (esophageal varices) or sclerotherapy; balloon tamponade (Sengstaken-Blakemore) as temporizing measure if uncontrolled
  5. TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) if refractory to endoscopic/medical therapy
- Secondary prophylaxis (after a bleed): non-selective beta-blockers + endoscopic band ligation combined (more effective than either alone)

## Ascites
- Diagnostic paracentesis in new-onset ascites: SAAG (serum-ascites albumin gradient) — ≥1.1 g/dL suggests portal hypertension-related (transudate-type mechanism); <1.1 g/dL suggests other causes (malignancy, TB peritonitis, pancreatitis)
- Management: sodium restriction, diuretics (spironolactone ± furosemide, typically in 100:40 mg ratio to maintain normokalemia), large volume paracentesis with albumin replacement for tense/refractory ascites, TIPS for refractory ascites

### Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP)
- Infection of ascitic fluid without an evident intra-abdominal surgical source
- Diagnosis: ascitic fluid absolute neutrophil (PMN) count ≥250 cells/mm³
- Common organism: E. coli (gut translocation)
- Management: empirical third-generation cephalosporin (cefotaxime); IV albumin reduces risk of hepatorenal syndrome in high-risk patients (high bilirubin/creatinine)
- Prophylaxis: long-term antibiotics (norfloxacin) in high-risk patients (prior SBP, low ascitic protein with additional risk factors, variceal bleed)

## Hepatic Encephalopathy
- Precipitants: infection, GI bleeding, constipation, electrolyte disturbance, sedatives, dehydration, excess dietary protein
- Grading: West Haven criteria (grade I mild confusion → grade IV coma)
- Clinical: asterixis (flapping tremor), confusion, altered sleep-wake cycle, fetor hepaticus
- Management: identify and correct precipitant, lactulose (first-line — reduces ammonia absorption via acidification/catharsis), rifaximin (added for recurrent/refractory cases — reduces ammonia-producing gut flora)

## Hepatorenal Syndrome
- Functional renal failure in advanced cirrhosis/portal hypertension due to severe renal vasoconstriction (splanchnic vasodilation → effective hypovolemia → renal hypoperfusion), diagnosis of exclusion (rule out other causes of AKI)
- Management: albumin + vasoconstrictors (terlipressin preferred where available; midodrine + octreotide alternative), definitive treatment is liver transplantation

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Cirrhosis is the most common cause of intrahepatic portal hypertension
- SAAG ≥1.1 g/dL indicates portal hypertension as the cause of ascites; <1.1 g/dL suggests malignancy, TB, or pancreatitis
- SBP is diagnosed by ascitic fluid PMN count ≥250 cells/mm³; cefotaxime is first-line empirical treatment
- Lactulose is first-line for hepatic encephalopathy; rifaximin is added for recurrent episodes
- Non-selective beta-blockers reduce risk of first variceal bleed and rebleeding — combined with band ligation for secondary prophylaxis
- Terlipressin/octreotide should be started as early as possible in suspected variceal bleeding, even before confirmatory endoscopy
- MELD score is used for liver transplant prioritization; Child-Pugh score estimates prognosis and guides clinical decisions
- IV albumin after large-volume paracentesis and in SBP with renal risk factors reduces risk of hepatorenal syndrome
