# Acute Pancreatitis

## Etiology
| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Gallstones | Most common cause overall (especially in women) |
| Alcohol | Second most common cause overall (especially in men); also a leading cause of chronic pancreatitis |
| Hypertriglyceridemia | TG typically >1000 mg/dL — important reversible cause |
| Post-ERCP | Common iatrogenic cause |
| Drugs | Thiazides, azathioprine, valproate, others |
| Hypercalcemia | Less common but classic association |
| Scorpion sting, trauma, idiopathic | — |

Mnemonic for causes: **"I GET SMASHED"** — Idiopathic, Gallstones, Ethanol, Trauma, Steroids, Mumps (and other infections), Autoimmune, Scorpion sting, Hypertriglyceridemia/Hypercalcemia, ERCP, Drugs

## Pathophysiology
Premature activation of pancreatic enzymes (trypsinogen → trypsin) within the pancreas → autodigestion → local inflammation → can progress to systemic inflammatory response, third-spacing, and multiorgan dysfunction in severe cases.

## Clinical Features
- Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, relieved by leaning forward, worsened by lying supine/eating
- Nausea, vomiting, low-grade fever, tachycardia
- Severe cases: Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising), Grey Turner's sign (flank bruising) — both indicate retroperitoneal hemorrhage/hemorrhagic pancreatitis
- Signs of hypocalcemia (Chvostek's/Trousseau's) can occur due to saponification of calcium with released fat

## Diagnosis — Requires 2 of 3
1. Characteristic abdominal pain
2. Serum lipase/amylase >3x upper limit of normal (lipase preferred — more sensitive and specific, remains elevated longer than amylase)
3. Characteristic imaging findings (CT/MRI/USG)

*High-yield: Lipase is preferred over amylase — amylase rises and normalizes faster and is less specific (can be elevated in many non-pancreatic conditions); lipase stays elevated longer, aiding delayed presentations.*

## Severity Assessment
- Scoring systems: Ranson's criteria, APACHE II, BISAP — used to predict severity/mortality
- Revised Atlanta classification: mild (no organ failure/complications), moderately severe (transient organ failure <48h and/or local complications), severe (persistent organ failure >48h)
- Markers of severity: hypocalcemia, rising BUN/creatinine despite fluids, hemoconcentration (rising hematocrit), CRP >150 at 48h, organ failure

## Investigations
- Serum lipase/amylase
- LFTs (elevated ALT suggests gallstone etiology), triglycerides, calcium
- Contrast-enhanced CT abdomen: best for assessing necrosis/complications, but ideally delayed 48–72h from onset (early CT may underestimate necrosis) unless diagnosis uncertain or complications suspected early
- USG abdomen: to assess for gallstones (etiology workup)

## Management

### Initial (All Patients)
- Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (crystalloids) — cornerstone of early management, improves outcomes
- Analgesia (opioids, historically avoided morphine due to sphincter of Oddi spasm concern — largely not clinically significant, most agents acceptable now)
- Bowel rest initially; early enteral feeding (oral/enteral, as tolerated) once feasible — early feeding improves outcomes vs prolonged fasting (major practice shift from older "nil by mouth until pain-free" teaching)
- Monitor for and treat electrolyte abnormalities (calcium, magnesium)

### Etiology-Specific
- Gallstone pancreatitis: ERCP (if concurrent cholangitis/obstruction), cholecystectomy during same admission for mild cases (reduces recurrence risk) — index admission cholecystectomy favored per updated evidence
- Hypertriglyceridemia-induced: insulin infusion ± plasmapheresis in severe cases, fibrates once stable
- Alcohol-related: counseling/cessation support

### Severe/Complicated Pancreatitis
- ICU-level care for organ dysfunction
- Antibiotics NOT routinely indicated prophylactically (reserved for confirmed infected necrosis/extrapancreatic infection) — key exam point, differs from older practice of routine prophylactic antibiotics
- Infected pancreatic necrosis: image-guided/minimally invasive drainage preferred over early open surgical debridement where feasible; delay intervention if possible to allow necrosis to organize (walled-off necrosis) unless clinical deterioration demands earlier action

## Complications
| Timing | Complication |
|---|---|
| Early (<4 weeks) | Acute peripancreatic fluid collection, acute necrotic collection, organ failure, ARDS, hypocalcemia |
| Late (>4 weeks) | Pancreatic pseudocyst (encapsulated fluid), walled-off necrosis, infected necrosis, splenic/portal vein thrombosis, pancreatic abscess |

- Pancreatic pseudocyst: managed conservatively if asymptomatic/small; drainage (endoscopic preferred) if symptomatic, growing, or complicated (infection, rupture, bleeding)

## Chronic Pancreatitis (Brief Contrast)
- Most common cause: chronic alcohol use (adults); cystic fibrosis (children)
- Clinical: recurrent/chronic pain, exocrine insufficiency (steatorrhea, malabsorption), endocrine insufficiency (diabetes)
- Imaging: pancreatic calcifications (classic), ductal dilation
- Management: pain control, pancreatic enzyme replacement, diabetes management, cessation of alcohol

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Gallstones and alcohol are the two most common causes of acute pancreatitis
- Diagnosis requires 2 of 3: characteristic pain, lipase/amylase >3x normal, characteristic imaging — lipase is the preferred enzyme
- Cullen's sign (periumbilical) and Grey Turner's sign (flank) indicate hemorrhagic/severe pancreatitis
- Aggressive early IV fluid resuscitation and early enteral feeding are the cornerstones of modern management
- Routine prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated in acute pancreatitis unless infected necrosis or extrapancreatic infection is confirmed
- Delay contrast CT to 48-72 hours (unless diagnosis uncertain) for optimal assessment of necrosis
- Cholecystectomy during the same admission is now favored for mild gallstone pancreatitis to prevent recurrence
- Hypertriglyceridemia (TG typically >1000 mg/dL) is an important reversible cause treated with insulin infusion ± plasmapheresis
