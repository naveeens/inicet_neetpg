# Pneumonia Pleural Effusion

## Pneumonia

### Classification
| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| Community-acquired (CAP) | Acquired outside hospital/healthcare setting |
| Hospital-acquired (HAP) | Onset ≥48 hours after admission |
| Ventilator-associated (VAP) | Onset ≥48 hours after intubation |
| Aspiration pneumonia | Aspiration of oropharyngeal/gastric contents; right lower lobe classically affected (more vertical, wider right main bronchus) |

### Etiology
| Setting | Common organisms |
|---|---|
| CAP (typical) | Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common overall) |
| CAP (atypical) | Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila, Legionella — dry cough, extrapulmonary symptoms, patchy/interstitial infiltrate |
| Post-influenza | Staphylococcus aureus (classic association) |
| COPD/smokers | Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis |
| Alcoholics/aspiration | Klebsiella pneumoniae ("currant jelly" sputum, cavitary upper lobe) |
| HAP/VAP | Pseudomonas aeruginosa, MRSA, gram-negative rods (drug-resistant organisms more likely) |
| Immunocompromised | Pneumocystis jirovecii, CMV, fungal |

### Clinical Features
- Fever, cough (productive in typical, dry in atypical), pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea
- Signs of consolidation: dullness to percussion, ↑tactile fremitus, bronchial breath sounds, crackles, egophony

### Diagnosis
- Chest X-ray: lobar consolidation (typical bacterial), patchy/interstitial infiltrates (atypical/viral)
- Sputum Gram stain/culture, blood cultures (before antibiotics if possible)
- Urinary antigen tests (pneumococcal, Legionella) where available
- Severity scoring: CURB-65 (Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate ≥30, BP <90/60, age ≥65) — guides admission decision

### Management
| Severity | Typical approach |
|---|---|
| Outpatient, no comorbidities | Amoxicillin or macrolide/doxycycline |
| Outpatient with comorbidities / inpatient non-ICU | Beta-lactam + macrolide, or respiratory fluoroquinolone |
| ICU/severe CAP | Beta-lactam + macrolide or fluoroquinolone (broader empirical cover); consider MRSA/Pseudomonas cover if risk factors |

- Empirical regimens vary by local resistance patterns and national guidelines — verify against latest local antibiogram/guideline
- Duration typically 5–7 days for uncomplicated CAP (shorter courses now favored per updated evidence, provided clinical improvement) — verify against latest guideline
- Vaccination: pneumococcal and influenza vaccines reduce CAP incidence/severity in at-risk groups

## Lung Abscess
- Complication of aspiration pneumonia, often polymicrobial/anaerobic
- Clinical: foul-smelling sputum, prolonged fever, cavitary lesion with air-fluid level on imaging
- Management: prolonged antibiotics (weeks), postural drainage; surgery/drainage rarely needed

## Pleural Effusion

### Approach — Transudate vs Exudate (Light's Criteria)
Exudate if ANY of:
1. Pleural fluid protein / serum protein >0.5
2. Pleural fluid LDH / serum LDH >0.6
3. Pleural fluid LDH > 2/3 upper limit of normal serum LDH

| Transudate causes | Exudate causes |
|---|---|
| Heart failure (most common transudate cause) | Infection (parapneumonic, TB — very common exudate cause in India) |
| Cirrhosis (hepatic hydrothorax) | Malignancy |
| Nephrotic syndrome | Pulmonary embolism |
| Hypoalbuminemia | Pancreatitis, connective tissue disease |

### Additional Fluid Analysis (For Exudates)
- Cell count/differential: neutrophil-predominant (acute/parapneumonic), lymphocyte-predominant (TB, malignancy, chronic)
- Glucose: low in empyema, TB, malignancy, rheumatoid effusion
- pH: low (<7.2) in complicated parapneumonic effusion/empyema — suggests need for drainage
- ADA (adenosine deaminase): elevated in tuberculous pleural effusion — useful in high-TB-burden settings like India
- Cytology for malignant cells

### Parapneumonic Effusion / Empyema
- Uncomplicated parapneumonic effusion: sterile, resolves with antibiotics alone
- Complicated/empyema: pus or positive culture, low pH/glucose, high LDH — requires chest tube drainage ± fibrinolytics, in addition to antibiotics; surgical decortication if organized/loculated and refractory

### Management (General)
- Treat underlying cause
- Therapeutic thoracocentesis for symptomatic relief/large effusions
- Chest tube drainage for empyema/complicated parapneumonic effusion or large symptomatic effusions not resolving

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common cause of community-acquired pneumonia overall
- Post-influenza pneumonia classically implicates Staphylococcus aureus
- Klebsiella pneumonia classically produces "currant jelly" sputum with upper lobe cavitation, seen in alcoholics
- Aspiration pneumonia most commonly affects the right lower lobe due to bronchial anatomy
- CURB-65 score guides site-of-care decisions in CAP
- Light's criteria differentiate transudate from exudate; heart failure is the most common cause of transudative effusion
- Elevated pleural fluid ADA strongly suggests tuberculous pleural effusion, especially in high-burden settings
- Low pleural fluid pH/glucose with high LDH indicates a complicated parapneumonic effusion requiring drainage
