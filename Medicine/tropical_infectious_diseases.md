# Tropical Infectious Diseases

## Malaria

### Etiology
Plasmodium species (falciparum, vivax, ovale, malariae, knowlesi) via female Anopheles mosquito. P. falciparum causes most severe/fatal disease; P. vivax most common in India historically (falciparum proportion rising in many regions).

### Clinical Features
- Classic paroxysm: cold stage (rigors) → hot stage (high fever) → sweating stage — cyclical (tertian ~48h for vivax/ovale, quartan ~72h for malariae; falciparum often less regular)
- Severe/complicated falciparum malaria: cerebral malaria (altered consciousness/seizures), severe anemia, acute kidney injury (blackwater fever — hemoglobinuria), ARDS, hypoglycemia, shock, DIC, jaundice

### Diagnosis
- Peripheral blood smear (thick smear for detection, thin smear for species identification) — gold standard
- Rapid diagnostic tests (antigen-based, e.g., HRP-2 for falciparum)

### Management
| Type | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Uncomplicated P. falciparum | Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) — e.g., artesunate + lumefantrine/other partner drug |
| Uncomplicated P. vivax/ovale | Chloroquine + primaquine (primaquine for radical cure — eliminates hepatic hypnozoites, prevents relapse; check G6PD status before primaquine — risk of hemolysis) |
| Severe/complicated malaria (any species) | IV Artesunate (drug of choice, superior to quinine in mortality — landmark trial evidence) |

*High-yield: Always check G6PD status before giving primaquine — hemolysis risk in G6PD deficiency.*

## Dengue

### Etiology
Dengue virus (flavivirus), 4 serotypes, transmitted by Aedes aegypti (day-biting mosquito).

### Clinical Phases
1. Febrile phase (2–7 days): high fever, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia ("breakbone fever"), rash
2. Critical phase (around defervescence, day 3–7): risk of plasma leakage/shock (dengue hemorrhagic fever/dengue shock syndrome) — this is when warning signs matter most
3. Recovery phase: reabsorption of fluid, may show classic "isles of white in a sea of red" confluent rash

### Warning Signs (Watch for Progression)
Abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, liver enlargement, rising hematocrit with falling platelets

### Diagnosis
- NS1 antigen (positive early, first 1–5 days)
- IgM/IgG serology (IgM rises after ~day 5)
- CBC: thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, rising hematocrit (hemoconcentration — indicates plasma leakage)

### Management
- Supportive — no specific antiviral
- Careful fluid management (avoid over- or under-hydration — critical in leak phase)
- Avoid NSAIDs/aspirin (bleeding risk) — use paracetamol for fever
- Platelet transfusion only if significant bleeding or very low counts with bleeding risk per clinical protocol (not for asymptomatic thrombocytopenia alone)

## Enteric (Typhoid) Fever

### Etiology
Salmonella Typhi/Paratyphi — fecal-oral transmission (contaminated food/water)

### Clinical Features
- Stepladder fever pattern (classic description), relative bradycardia (pulse-temperature dissociation), abdominal pain, rose spots (trunk), hepatosplenomegaly, constipation early → diarrhea later
- Complications: intestinal perforation/hemorrhage (classically 3rd week), typhoid encephalopathy

### Diagnosis
- Blood culture: gold standard, most sensitive in first week
- Bone marrow culture: most sensitive overall (even after antibiotics started) but not routinely used
- Widal test: low sensitivity/specificity, largely historical, still asked in exams

### Management
- Antibiotics: ceftriaxone (empirical, given rising fluoroquinolone resistance) or azithromycin for uncomplicated cases; fluoroquinolones if known susceptible
- Supportive care, monitor for perforation

## Leptospirosis
- Spirochete (Leptospira), transmitted via contact with water/soil contaminated by animal urine (rats classic reservoir) — occupational/flood-related exposure
- Clinical: biphasic illness — leptospiremic phase (fever, myalgia, conjunctival suffusion — classic sign) → immune phase (Weil's disease: jaundice + AKI + hemorrhage in severe form)
- Diagnosis: serology (MAT — microscopic agglutination test, gold standard), PCR early
- Management: doxycycline (mild), IV penicillin/ceftriaxone (severe)

## Scrub Typhus
- Orientia tsutsugamushi, transmitted by chigger mite bite
- Clinical: fever, eschar (pathognomonic but not always present) at bite site, lymphadenopathy, rash; can progress to multiorgan dysfunction
- Diagnosis: Weil-Felix test (historical), IgM ELISA (more specific)
- Management: doxycycline (drug of choice, all ages including children for this indication given severity); azithromycin alternative (pregnancy)

## Chikungunya
- Alphavirus, Aedes-transmitted
- Clinical: high fever + severe polyarthralgia/arthritis (often symmetric, small joints) — arthralgia can persist for weeks-months post-infection
- Management: supportive, NSAIDs for joint pain (once dengue co-infection/hemorrhagic risk excluded)

## Kala-azar (Visceral Leishmaniasis)
- Leishmania donovani, transmitted by sandfly (Phlebotomus)
- Clinical: prolonged fever, massive hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, weight loss, hyperpigmentation of skin ("kala-azar" = black fever/black sickness)
- Diagnosis: bone marrow/splenic aspirate showing amastigotes (Leishman-Donovan bodies); rK39 rapid antigen test
- Management: liposomal amphotericin B (drug of choice in India per national program), miltefosine as alternative

## High-Yield One-Liners
- IV artesunate is the drug of choice for severe/complicated malaria — superior to quinine in mortality benefit
- Always check G6PD status before giving primaquine (radical cure for P. vivax/ovale)
- Dengue: NS1 antigen positive early, IgM appears after ~day 5; rising hematocrit + falling platelets signals plasma leak
- Avoid NSAIDs/aspirin in suspected dengue — use paracetamol due to bleeding risk
- Typhoid fever: relative bradycardia with high fever is a classic exam clue; intestinal perforation classically occurs in the third week
- Leptospirosis: conjunctival suffusion is a classic bedside clue; Weil's disease = jaundice + AKI + bleeding
- Scrub typhus: look for an eschar at the mite bite site; doxycycline is the treatment of choice
- Chikungunya causes prominent, often prolonged polyarthralgia, distinguishing it clinically from dengue
- Kala-azar: massive hepatosplenomegaly + pancytopenia + prolonged fever — think visceral leishmaniasis, confirm with bone marrow amastigotes or rK39 test
