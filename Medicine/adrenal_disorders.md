# Adrenal Disorders

## Adrenal Cortex Physiology
- Zona glomerulosa → mineralocorticoids (aldosterone)
- Zona fasciculata → glucocorticoids (cortisol)
- Zona reticularis → androgens (DHEA)
- Mnemonic: "GFR — salt, sugar, sex" (deeper you go, sweeter it gets)

## Cushing Syndrome

### Etiology
| Cause | Feature |
|---|---|
| Iatrogenic (exogenous steroids) | Most common cause overall |
| Pituitary adenoma (Cushing's disease) | Most common cause of endogenous Cushing syndrome; ACTH-dependent |
| Ectopic ACTH (small cell lung cancer, carcinoid) | ACTH-dependent |
| Adrenal adenoma/carcinoma | ACTH-independent |

### Clinical Features
- Central obesity, moon facies, buffalo hump, purple striae, easy bruising, proximal muscle weakness, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hirsutism/acne (if androgen excess), osteoporosis, psychiatric disturbance

### Diagnosis (Stepwise)
1. Confirm hypercortisolism: overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test, OR 24-hour urinary free cortisol, OR late-night salivary cortisol (any of these as first-line screening)
2. Localize: plasma ACTH — low/suppressed suggests adrenal source; high/normal suggests pituitary or ectopic source
3. High-dose dexamethasone suppression test / CRH stimulation test to differentiate pituitary (suppresses/responds) from ectopic (doesn't suppress/respond)
4. Imaging: pituitary MRI, adrenal CT, or chest CT (ectopic source) as guided by biochemical localization

### Management
- Pituitary source: transsphenoidal surgery
- Adrenal adenoma: adrenalectomy
- Ectopic: treat primary tumor
- Medical therapy (ketoconazole, metyrapone) if surgery not feasible/as bridge

## Adrenal Insufficiency

### Etiology
| Type | Cause |
|---|---|
| Primary (Addison's disease) | Autoimmune adrenalitis (most common in developed world), TB (classic in India — high-yield), metastasis, hemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen in meningococcemia) |
| Secondary | Pituitary disease, exogenous steroid withdrawal (most common cause of secondary insufficiency overall) |

### Clinical Features
| Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|
| Hyperpigmentation (↑ACTH → ↑MSH precursor) | No hyperpigmentation |
| Hyperkalemia + hyponatremia (aldosterone deficiency too) | Usually normal K+ (aldosterone axis intact) |
| Both glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid deficiency | Glucocorticoid deficiency only |

- Common to both: fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, GI symptoms

### Investigations
- Morning serum cortisol (low), ACTH (high in primary, low/normal in secondary)
- ACTH (cosyntropin) stimulation test: no rise in primary adrenal insufficiency; some rise possible in secondary (adrenal gland intact)
- Anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies (autoimmune), CT adrenal (TB — enlarged/calcified glands), imaging pituitary if secondary suspected

### Addisonian Crisis
- Precipitated by stress, infection, surgery in undiagnosed/undertreated patient, or steroid withdrawal
- Features: severe hypotension/shock, abdominal pain, vomiting, altered mentation
- Management: IV hydrocortisone immediately (do not wait for confirmatory tests if crisis suspected), aggressive IV fluids (normal saline + dextrose), correct hypoglycemia/hyperkalemia, treat precipitating cause

### Management (Chronic)
- Glucocorticoid replacement (hydrocortisone) + mineralocorticoid replacement (fludrocortisone) if primary
- Patient education: stress-dose steroids during illness/surgery, medical alert card

## Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn Syndrome)
- Aldosterone-producing adrenal adenoma (or bilateral hyperplasia) → hypertension + hypokalemia + metabolic alkalosis + suppressed renin
- Screening: plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio (elevated ratio suggests primary hyperaldosteronism)
- Confirmatory: saline suppression test
- Localization: adrenal CT, adrenal vein sampling (differentiates unilateral adenoma from bilateral hyperplasia — guides surgery vs medical therapy)
- Management: unilateral adenoma → laparoscopic adrenalectomy; bilateral hyperplasia → mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (spironolactone/eplerenone)

## Pheochromocytoma
- Catecholamine-secreting tumor of adrenal medulla (chromaffin cells); "Rule of 10s" (10% bilateral, 10% extra-adrenal, 10% malignant, 10% familial — figures approximate, classic teaching)
- Associated with MEN 2, von Hippel-Lindau, neurofibromatosis type 1
- Clinical: episodic headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, hypertension (paroxysmal or sustained)
- Diagnosis: plasma free metanephrines/24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines (screening test of choice); CT/MRI abdomen for localization; MIBG scan for extra-adrenal/metastatic disease
- Management: surgical resection after adequate alpha-blockade (phenoxybenzamine) followed by beta-blockade preoperatively — never start beta-blocker before alpha-blockade (unopposed alpha-mediated vasoconstriction → hypertensive crisis)

## Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)
- Most common: 21-hydroxylase deficiency → ↓cortisol, ↓aldosterone (in severe form), ↑androgens (shunted pathway)
- Classic salt-wasting form: presents in neonate with vomiting, dehydration, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, ambiguous genitalia in females (virilization)
- Diagnosis: elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone
- Management: glucocorticoid ± mineralocorticoid replacement

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Exogenous steroid use = most common cause of Cushing syndrome overall; pituitary adenoma = most common endogenous cause
- Hyperpigmentation differentiates primary (Addison's) from secondary adrenal insufficiency
- TB is a classic cause of primary adrenal insufficiency in India — look for calcified enlarged adrenals on CT
- Never delay hydrocortisone administration for suspected Addisonian crisis while awaiting lab confirmation
- Conn syndrome: hypertension + hypokalemia + metabolic alkalosis + low renin/high aldosterone
- Pheochromocytoma: always alpha-block before beta-block prior to surgery/definitive treatment
- 21-hydroxylase deficiency is the most common cause of congenital adrenal hyperplasia; presents with salt-wasting and virilization
- Plasma free metanephrines is the preferred screening test for pheochromocytoma
