# Viral Hepatitis

## Overview of Hepatitis Viruses
| Virus | Genome | Transmission | Chronicity | Vaccine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hepatitis A (HAV) | RNA (picornavirus) | Fecal-oral | No | Yes |
| Hepatitis B (HBV) | DNA (hepadnavirus) | Blood, sexual, vertical | Yes | Yes |
| Hepatitis C (HCV) | RNA (flavivirus) | Blood (IV drug use, transfusion) | Yes (high rate) | No |
| Hepatitis D (HDV) | RNA (defective, needs HBV) | Blood, sexual (co-/superinfection with HBV) | Yes (with HBV) | No (protected by HBV vaccine) |
| Hepatitis E (HEV) | RNA (hepevirus) | Fecal-oral (contaminated water) | Usually no (except immunocompromised); severe in pregnancy | Limited availability |

*High-yield: Hepatitis E in pregnancy carries a high risk of fulminant hepatic failure and maternal mortality — a classic exam point, especially relevant in India given HEV's endemic waterborne transmission.*

## Hepatitis B — Serology (High-Yield)
| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| HBsAg | Current infection (acute or chronic) — first marker to appear |
| Anti-HBs | Immunity (past infection resolved OR vaccination) |
| HBcAg | Not detectable in serum (intracellular) |
| Anti-HBc IgM | Acute infection |
| Anti-HBc IgG | Past or chronic infection (persists lifelong) |
| HBeAg | Active viral replication, high infectivity |
| Anti-HBe | Seroconversion, lower replication (usually) |
| HBV DNA | Direct measure of viral replication/load |

- "Window period": HBsAg has cleared but anti-HBs not yet risen — anti-HBc IgM is the only positive marker, confirming recent infection
- Vaccinated (not infected): anti-HBs positive only (HBsAg negative, anti-HBc negative)
- Resolved natural infection: anti-HBs AND anti-HBc (IgG) both positive; HBsAg negative

## Hepatitis B — Natural History / Chronicity Risk
- Risk of chronicity is inversely related to age at infection: neonatal/perinatal infection → >90% chronicity; adult infection → <5% chronicity (immune system maturity determines clearance)
- Chronic HBV → risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HBV can cause HCC even without cirrhosis, unlike most other causes)

## Hepatitis C
- Often asymptomatic acute infection; majority (~75-85%) progress to chronic infection
- Chronic HCV → cirrhosis, HCC risk (usually via cirrhosis pathway)
- Diagnosis: anti-HCV antibody (screening) → HCV RNA (confirms active infection, since antibody persists even after clearance/cure)
- Treatment: direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) — sofosbuvir-based regimens etc., >90-95% cure rates, revolutionized management (major practice change from older interferon-based regimens) — regimen choice depends on genotype/cirrhosis status, verify against latest guideline

## Clinical Features (Acute Viral Hepatitis, General)
- Prodrome: fatigue, nausea, anorexia, low-grade fever, right upper quadrant discomfort
- Icteric phase: jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus (cholestatic component), hepatomegaly/tenderness
- Recovery phase: resolution over weeks

## Investigations
- LFTs: markedly elevated transaminases (ALT often > AST in viral hepatitis, opposite pattern in alcoholic hepatitis where AST > ALT); bilirubin elevated
- Specific serologies as above per suspected virus
- Coagulation profile (PT/INR) — key marker of severity; marked prolongation suggests fulminant hepatic failure

## Management
- Most acute viral hepatitis (A, B, E in immunocompetent, non-pregnant) — supportive care, self-limiting
- Acute HBV: usually supportive; antivirals reserved for severe/fulminant cases or specific risk factors
- Chronic HBV: antiviral therapy (tenofovir, entecavir — preferred nucleos(t)ide analogues) indicated based on HBV DNA level, ALT, and fibrosis stage — verify against latest treatment threshold guideline
- Chronic HCV: DAA therapy for essentially all diagnosed patients (regardless of fibrosis stage, per current guidance)
- Fulminant hepatic failure: supportive/ICU care, evaluate early for liver transplantation

## Prevention
- Hepatitis A & B vaccines are part of routine immunization in many national schedules
- HBV post-exposure prophylaxis: hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) + vaccine series (e.g., neonates born to HBsAg-positive mothers, needlestick exposure in unvaccinated individuals)
- Safe blood transfusion practices, harm reduction (needle exchange) reduce HBV/HCV transmission
- Safe drinking water reduces HAV/HEV transmission

## Complications
- Chronic hepatitis (B, C) → cirrhosis → hepatocellular carcinoma
- Fulminant hepatic failure (more common with HBV, HEV in pregnancy, or hepatitis A in older patients with underlying liver disease)
- Extrahepatic manifestations: HBV — polyarteritis nodosa, membranous nephropathy; HCV — cryoglobulinemia, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Anti-HBc IgM is the key marker of the "window period" in acute HBV infection when HBsAg has cleared but anti-HBs has not yet appeared
- Isolated anti-HBs positivity indicates vaccination (not infection); anti-HBs + anti-HBc together indicate resolved natural infection
- Risk of chronic HBV infection is highest with perinatal/neonatal transmission and lowest with adult-acquired infection
- Hepatitis E in pregnancy carries a high risk of fulminant hepatic failure and mortality
- HCV diagnosis requires HCV RNA confirmation since anti-HCV antibody persists even after viral clearance
- Direct-acting antivirals have made chronic hepatitis C a curable disease in the vast majority of patients
- HBV can cause hepatocellular carcinoma even in the absence of cirrhosis, unlike most other chronic liver diseases
- AST > ALT pattern suggests alcoholic hepatitis; ALT > AST is more typical of viral hepatitis
