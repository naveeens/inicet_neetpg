# Anemia

## Approach — By MCV
| Type | MCV | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| Microcytic | <80 fL | Iron deficiency (most common overall), thalassemia, chronic disease (can also be normocytic), sideroblastic anemia, lead poisoning |
| Normocytic | 80–100 fL | Acute blood loss, anemia of chronic disease, hemolysis (early), aplastic anemia, chronic kidney disease |
| Macrocytic | >100 fL | Vitamin B12/folate deficiency (megaloblastic), hypothyroidism, liver disease, alcohol, reticulocytosis (hemolysis/blood loss recovery), myelodysplastic syndrome |

## Iron Deficiency Anemia
- Most common cause of anemia worldwide
- Causes: chronic blood loss (GI — think colon cancer in older adults; menorrhagia in women), poor dietary intake, malabsorption (celiac disease), increased demand (pregnancy)
- Clinical: fatigue, pallor, koilonychia (spoon nails), pica, angular stomatitis, glossitis, Plummer-Vinson syndrome (dysphagia + esophageal web + iron deficiency anemia)
- Labs: ↓ferritin (most specific — but is an acute phase reactant, can be falsely normal/elevated in inflammation), ↓serum iron, ↑TIBC, ↓transferrin saturation, high RDW, microcytic hypochromic RBCs on smear
- Management: oral iron supplementation (ferrous sulfate) — continue for 3-6 months after Hb normalizes to replenish stores; IV iron if intolerant/malabsorption/ongoing losses exceeding oral replacement capacity; always investigate underlying cause of blood loss in adults (especially colon cancer screening in older patients with iron deficiency anemia)

## Thalassemia
- Alpha or beta globin chain synthesis defect
- Beta-thalassemia major: severe anemia presenting in infancy, requires regular transfusions, extramedullary hematopoiesis (hepatosplenomegaly, "chipmunk facies" from marrow expansion), iron overload from transfusions (needs chelation — deferoxamine/deferasirox)
- Beta-thalassemia minor (trait): mild/asymptomatic microcytic anemia, often mistaken for iron deficiency — key distinguishing point: normal/high ferritin and iron studies, elevated HbA2 on electrophoresis
- Diagnosis: hemoglobin electrophoresis

*High-yield: Differentiate iron deficiency anemia from thalassemia trait using the Mentzer index (MCV/RBC count) — a lower ratio suggests thalassemia trait (disproportionately high RBC count for the degree of microcytosis), and confirm with iron studies/electrophoresis; never treat thalassemia trait with iron unless deficiency is proven.*

## Megaloblastic Anemia (B12/Folate Deficiency)
| Feature | B12 deficiency | Folate deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Causes | Pernicious anemia (autoimmune, anti-intrinsic factor/anti-parietal cell antibodies), vegan diet, malabsorption (terminal ileum disease), post-gastrectomy | Poor diet, alcoholism, pregnancy (increased demand), certain drugs (methotrexate, phenytoin) |
| Neurological involvement | Yes — subacute combined degeneration (dorsal columns + corticospinal tracts + peripheral nerves) | No neurological involvement |
| Onset | Slow (years, given large hepatic B12 stores) | Faster (weeks-months, minimal folate stores) |

- Labs: macrocytic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, elevated MCV, elevated homocysteine (both), elevated methylmalonic acid (B12 deficiency specific — helps differentiate)
- Management: B12 replacement (IM/oral depending on cause — pernicious anemia typically needs parenteral or high-dose oral); folate replacement — always rule out/treat B12 deficiency before/concurrently with folate in combined deficiency (giving folate alone can worsen neurological symptoms of undiagnosed B12 deficiency while correcting the anemia — "masking" phenomenon)

## Anemia of Chronic Disease
- Chronic inflammation/infection/malignancy → hepcidin-mediated iron sequestration → functional iron deficiency
- Labs: normocytic (can become microcytic), ↓serum iron, ↓TIBC (differentiates from true iron deficiency where TIBC is high), normal/high ferritin (acute phase reactant)
- Management: treat underlying disease; iron supplementation generally not helpful unless true co-existing iron deficiency

## Hemolytic Anemias
| Type | Examples | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Hereditary spherocytosis | Membrane defect (spectrin/ankyrin) | Spherocytes, increased osmotic fragility, splenomegaly, splenectomy often curative |
| G6PD deficiency | X-linked, oxidative stress-triggered hemolysis | Triggered by oxidant drugs (sulfa, primaquine, dapsone), fava beans, infection; Heinz bodies, bite cells |
| Sickle cell disease | HbS mutation (autosomal recessive) | Vaso-occlusive crises, dactylitis, autosplenectomy, increased infection risk (encapsulated organisms), acute chest syndrome |
| Autoimmune hemolytic anemia | Warm (IgG, extravascular, spherocytes) vs cold (IgM, intravascular, complement-mediated) | Positive direct Coombs test |
| Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia | TTP, HUS, DIC, malignant hypertension | Schistocytes on smear |

- Common findings in hemolysis (any cause): ↑reticulocyte count, ↑indirect bilirubin, ↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin
- Direct Coombs test (direct antiglobulin test) positive in autoimmune/immune-mediated hemolysis; negative in hereditary/mechanical causes

## Aplastic Anemia
- Bone marrow failure → pancytopenia (anemia + leukopenia + thrombocytopenia)
- Causes: idiopathic (most common), drugs (chloramphenicol, chemotherapy), radiation, viral infections (parvovirus B19 — especially in sickle cell/hemolytic disease causing aplastic crisis), Fanconi anemia (inherited)
- Diagnosis: bone marrow biopsy shows hypocellular marrow with fatty replacement
- Management: supportive care (transfusions), immunosuppression (antithymocyte globulin + cyclosporine), allogeneic bone marrow transplant (curative, preferred in younger patients with matched donor)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Iron deficiency anemia is the most common cause of anemia worldwide; ferritin is the most specific test but can be falsely elevated in inflammation
- Mentzer index helps distinguish iron deficiency anemia (higher ratio) from thalassemia trait (lower ratio)
- B12 deficiency causes neurological deficits (subacute combined degeneration); folate deficiency does not
- Never treat isolated folate deficiency without first excluding/treating B12 deficiency — can precipitate/worsen neurological damage
- Anemia of chronic disease shows low TIBC (unlike true iron deficiency, where TIBC is high) with normal/high ferritin
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear point to microangiopathic hemolytic processes (TTP, HUS, DIC)
- Direct Coombs test differentiates immune-mediated hemolysis (positive) from hereditary/mechanical hemolysis (negative)
- G6PD deficiency hemolysis is classically triggered by oxidant drugs, fava beans, or infection — look for Heinz bodies and bite cells
