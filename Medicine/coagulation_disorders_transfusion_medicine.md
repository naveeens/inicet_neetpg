# Coagulation Disorders Transfusion Medicine

## Approach to Bleeding Disorders
| Test | Pathway assessed |
|---|---|
| PT (prothrombin time) | Extrinsic + common pathway (factors VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) |
| aPTT (activated partial thromboplastin time) | Intrinsic + common pathway (factors VIII, IX, XI, XII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) |
| Bleeding time / platelet function tests | Platelet number/function, von Willebrand factor |
| Thrombin time | Fibrinogen conversion to fibrin |

| Pattern | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Isolated ↑PT | Factor VII deficiency, early liver disease, warfarin effect, vitamin K deficiency (early) |
| Isolated ↑aPTT | Hemophilia A/B, von Willebrand disease, factor XI/XII deficiency, lupus anticoagulant (paradoxically prolongs aPTT despite being prothrombotic clinically) |
| Both ↑PT and ↑aPTT | Liver disease (advanced), DIC, vitamin K deficiency (advanced), common pathway factor deficiency, warfarin (higher dose/overdose), heparin (high dose) |
| Normal PT/aPTT with bleeding | Platelet disorder (quantitative or qualitative), von Willebrand disease (can have normal or prolonged aPTT), factor XIII deficiency, vascular/connective tissue disorder |

## Hemophilia
- X-linked recessive; Hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency, more common), Hemophilia B (factor IX deficiency, "Christmas disease")
- Clinical: hemarthrosis (classic — recurrent joint bleeds leading to arthropathy), deep muscle hematomas, prolonged bleeding after surgery/trauma; mucocutaneous bleeding less typical (differentiates from platelet disorders/vWD)
- Labs: isolated prolonged aPTT, normal PT, normal platelet count/bleeding time; confirm with specific factor assay
- Management: factor replacement (recombinant factor VIII/IX) — on-demand or prophylactic; desmopressin (DDAVP) can boost factor VIII release in mild Hemophilia A; emerging non-factor therapies (e.g., emicizumab) increasingly used for Hemophilia A prophylaxis — verify against latest guideline

## Von Willebrand Disease
- Most common inherited bleeding disorder overall
- vWF deficiency/dysfunction → impaired platelet adhesion + secondary factor VIII deficiency (vWF stabilizes factor VIII)
- Clinical: mucocutaneous bleeding (epistaxis, gum bleeding, menorrhagia, easy bruising) — platelet-type bleeding pattern
- Labs: prolonged bleeding time, normal/prolonged aPTT (due to associated factor VIII reduction), normal platelet count, decreased vWF antigen/activity (ristocetin cofactor assay)
- Management: desmopressin (DDAVP) for mild type 1 disease (stimulates vWF release from endothelium); vWF-containing factor concentrates for severe disease/major bleeding/surgery

## Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)
- Widespread activation of coagulation cascade → consumption of clotting factors/platelets → simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis
- Causes: sepsis (most common), trauma, malignancy, obstetric complications (abruption, amniotic fluid embolism), massive transfusion, snake bite
- Labs: prolonged PT and aPTT, thrombocytopenia, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer/fibrin degradation products, schistocytes on peripheral smear (microangiopathic hemolysis)
- Management: treat underlying trigger (most important step), supportive transfusion of blood products (FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets) guided by bleeding/lab derangement — not simply based on lab numbers alone if patient is not bleeding

## Vitamin K Deficiency
- Vitamin K is required for gamma-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, X (and protein C/S)
- Causes: poor intake, malabsorption (biliary obstruction, fat malabsorption), broad-spectrum antibiotics (kill gut flora producing vitamin K), warfarin (functional vitamin K antagonism), hemorrhagic disease of the newborn (given prophylactically to neonates for this reason)
- Labs: prolonged PT initially (factor VII has shortest half-life, affected earliest), later prolonged aPTT too
- Management: vitamin K replacement (oral/IV/subcutaneous depending on urgency); fresh frozen plasma for urgent reversal of severe bleeding

## Platelet Disorders
- Thrombocytopenia causes: decreased production (marrow failure, aplastic anemia, marrow infiltration), increased destruction (ITP, TTP, HUS, DIC, hypersplenism), dilutional (massive transfusion)
- Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP): autoantibody-mediated platelet destruction; isolated thrombocytopenia, otherwise well patient; management — observation (if mild/asymptomatic), corticosteroids/IVIG first-line for significant bleeding risk, splenectomy or thrombopoietin receptor agonists for refractory/chronic cases
- TTP: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia + thrombocytopenia + (classically) neurological symptoms, renal dysfunction, fever (pentad rarely all present) — due to ADAMTS13 deficiency; medical emergency — plasma exchange is the treatment of choice, platelet transfusion generally avoided/contraindicated unless life-threatening bleeding (can worsen microthrombi)

## Transfusion Medicine

### Blood Products
| Product | Indication |
|---|---|
| Packed RBCs | Symptomatic anemia/blood loss — restrictive transfusion strategy generally preferred (target Hb ~7-8 g/dL in most stable patients) |
| Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) | Coagulation factor deficiencies (multiple factors), urgent warfarin reversal, DIC with active bleeding |
| Platelets | Thrombocytopenia with bleeding or prior to invasive procedures (threshold depends on procedure/bleeding risk) |
| Cryoprecipitate | Fibrinogen deficiency/hypofibrinogenemia, factor VIII/vWF/factor XIII deficiency when specific concentrates unavailable |

### Transfusion Reactions
| Reaction | Feature | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction | ABO incompatibility, fever, hemoglobinuria, back pain, hypotension — can be fatal | Stop transfusion immediately, supportive care, IV fluids |
| Febrile non-hemolytic reaction | Most common transfusion reaction overall; fever/chills without hemolysis, due to cytokines/leukocyte antibodies | Stop/slow transfusion, antipyretics, supportive |
| Allergic/anaphylactic reaction | Urticaria to anaphylaxis; IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies classic cause of severe anaphylactic reactions | Antihistamines for mild; stop transfusion + epinephrine for severe |
| Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) | Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema within 6 hours of transfusion | Stop transfusion, supportive respiratory care (similar to ARDS management) |
| Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO) | Cardiogenic pulmonary edema from volume overload, especially in cardiac/renal patients | Stop transfusion, diuretics, slow future transfusion rates |
| Delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction | Anamnestic antibody response to minor antigens, days after transfusion | Usually mild, monitor |

*High-yield: Febrile non-hemolytic reaction is the most common transfusion reaction overall; acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (ABO incompatibility) is the most dangerous and requires immediate cessation of transfusion.*

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Isolated prolonged aPTT with normal PT suggests hemophilia or von Willebrand disease; confirm with specific factor assays
- Von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder and shows a mucocutaneous ("platelet-type") bleeding pattern
- Hemophilia classically causes hemarthrosis and deep tissue bleeding, not mucocutaneous bleeding
- DIC shows prolonged PT/aPTT, thrombocytopenia, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer, and schistocytes — treat the underlying trigger first
- Factor VII has the shortest half-life among vitamin K-dependent factors, so PT prolongs first in early vitamin K deficiency/warfarin effect
- Plasma exchange, not platelet transfusion, is the treatment of choice for TTP
- Febrile non-hemolytic reaction is the most common transfusion reaction; acute hemolytic reaction (ABO mismatch) is the most dangerous
- A restrictive transfusion threshold (Hb ~7 g/dL) is preferred over liberal transfusion in most stable patients
