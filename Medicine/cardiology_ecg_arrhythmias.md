# Cardiology ECG Arrhythmias

## Normal ECG Basics
- Rate = 300/(number of large boxes between R-R); or 1500/(small boxes between R-R)
- PR interval: 0.12–0.20 s (3–5 small boxes)
- QRS: <0.12 s
- QTc (Bazett): <0.44 s (men), <0.46 s (women)
- Axis: normal −30° to +90°

## Approach to Arrhythmias
| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | Rate — fast (>100) or slow (<60)? |
| 2 | Rhythm — regular or irregular? |
| 3 | QRS — narrow (supraventricular) or wide (ventricular/aberrant conduction)? |
| 4 | P waves — present, absent, morphology, relation to QRS? |

## Bradyarrhythmias

### Sinus Node Dysfunction
- Sinus bradycardia: rate <60, normal P before every QRS — physiological in athletes; pathological with drugs (beta-blockers), hypothyroidism, ↑ICP
- Sick sinus syndrome: tachy-brady syndrome, common indication for pacemaker

### AV Blocks
| Type | ECG | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1st degree | PR >0.20 s, every P conducts | Benign, no treatment |
| 2nd degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach) | Progressive PR prolongation → dropped QRS | Usually benign, AV node level |
| 2nd degree Mobitz II | Fixed PR, sudden dropped QRS (no progressive lengthening) | Higher risk of progression to complete block — pacemaker indicated |
| 3rd degree (complete) | No relation between P and QRS, AV dissociation | Pacemaker indicated |

*High-yield: Mobitz II and complete heart block are indications for permanent pacemaker; Mobitz I usually is not (unless symptomatic).*

## Narrow-Complex Tachycardias (Supraventricular)

| Arrhythmia | ECG features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Sinus tachycardia | Rate >100, normal P-QRS | Treat underlying cause |
| Atrial fibrillation | Irregularly irregular, no discrete P waves, fibrillatory baseline | Rate control (beta-blocker/CCB) + anticoagulation (CHA₂DS₂-VASc) ± rhythm control |
| Atrial flutter | "Sawtooth" flutter waves, rate ~300, often 2:1 block (ventricular rate ~150) | Rate/rhythm control, anticoagulation as per AF |
| AVNRT (paroxysmal SVT) | Regular narrow complex, P buried in QRS or retrograde P | Vagal maneuvers → IV adenosine (drug of choice, transient AV block) |
| Multifocal atrial tachycardia | ≥3 different P wave morphologies, irregular | Treat underlying (classically COPD); CCBs |

*High-yield: Adenosine is both diagnostic and therapeutic for AVNRT — terminates the arrhythmia by transient AV nodal block; very short half-life (<10 s), give as rapid IV push with saline flush; caution in asthma (may cause bronchospasm) and avoid in WPW with pre-excited AF (can precipitate VF).*

## Atrial Fibrillation — Key Points
- Most common sustained arrhythmia
- Causes: hypertension, valvular heart disease (esp. mitral stenosis), hyperthyroidism, ischemic heart disease, sepsis, alcohol ("holiday heart")
- CHA₂DS₂-VASc score guides anticoagulation decision; HAS-BLED estimates bleeding risk
- Rhythm vs rate control: no clear mortality benefit of one over other in most patients (rate control often preferred, especially elderly); rhythm control favored in younger, symptomatic, or early-diagnosed AF (current evidence increasingly favors early rhythm control in select patients — verify against latest guideline)
- Anticoagulation: DOACs preferred over warfarin in non-valvular AF (except mechanical valves/significant mitral stenosis, where warfarin remains standard)

## Wide-Complex Tachycardias

| Feature | Ventricular Tachycardia | SVT with aberrancy |
|---|---|---|
| AV dissociation | Present | Absent |
| Fusion/capture beats | Present (highly specific for VT) | Absent |
| Concordance (all leads same direction V1-V6) | Suggests VT | — |
| Hemodynamic stability | Assume VT until proven otherwise | — |

*High-yield: In a wide-complex tachycardia of uncertain origin, always treat as VT until proven otherwise — do not give verapamil (can cause hemodynamic collapse in VT).*

### Ventricular Tachycardia/Fibrillation
- Sustained VT (>30 s or hemodynamically unstable): synchronized cardioversion if unstable; amiodarone/lidocaine if stable
- VF: immediate defibrillation (unsynchronized) + CPR per ACLS
- Torsades de pointes: polymorphic VT with prolonged QT; treat with IV magnesium sulfate (drug of choice) regardless of Mg level, correct electrolytes (K, Mg), stop offending drugs; overdrive pacing/isoproterenol if refractory

### Long QT Syndrome
- Congenital (Romano-Ward, Jervell-Lange-Nielsen with deafness) or acquired (drugs — antiarrhythmics, macrolides, antipsychotics, antiemetics; electrolyte: hypoK, hypoMg, hypoCa; bradycardia)
- Risk of torsades de pointes

## Pre-excitation — WPW Syndrome
- Accessory pathway (Bundle of Kent) bypasses AV node
- ECG: short PR interval, delta wave (slurred QRS upstroke), wide QRS
- Risk: AF with pre-excitation can degenerate to VF (avoid AV-nodal blocking drugs — digoxin, verapamil, adenosine — in pre-excited AF as they can accelerate conduction over the accessory pathway)
- Definitive treatment: radiofrequency catheter ablation of accessory pathway

## Pacemaker Basics
- Common indications: symptomatic sinus node dysfunction, Mobitz II, complete heart block, symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to reversible causes
- Pacemaker ECG: pacer spike before P wave (atrial pacing) or before QRS (ventricular pacing)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Adenosine = drug of choice for AVNRT (rapid IV push, half-life <10 seconds)
- Wide complex tachycardia → treat as VT until proven otherwise
- Torsades de pointes → IV magnesium sulfate is treatment of choice
- Mobitz II / complete heart block → pacemaker; Mobitz I usually observed
- Avoid AV-nodal blockers (digoxin, verapamil, adenosine) in WPW with pre-excited AF
- Fusion and capture beats on ECG are highly specific for VT
- CHA₂DS₂-VASc score determines need for anticoagulation in AF; HAS-BLED estimates bleeding risk
