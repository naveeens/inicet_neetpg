# Electrolyte Acid Base Disorders

## Sodium Disorders

### Hyponatremia
- Definition: serum Na+ <135 mEq/L
- Classify by volume status:
| Volume status | Causes |
|---|---|
| Hypovolemic | GI losses, diuretics (especially thiazides), Addison's disease |
| Euvolemic | SIADH (most common cause of euvolemic hyponatremia — malignancy, CNS disease, pulmonary disease, drugs), hypothyroidism, psychogenic polydipsia |
| Hypervolemic | Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome (all edematous states with reduced effective circulating volume despite total body Na+/water excess) |

- Clinical features correlate with rate of fall and severity: nausea, headache, confusion → seizures, coma (cerebral edema) if severe/rapid
- Management: correct underlying cause; rate of correction is critical — **do not correct faster than 8–10 mEq/L in 24 hours** (some guidelines say even more conservative, ~6-8 in high risk) — overly rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis), especially in chronic hyponatremia, alcoholics, malnourished patients
- Severe symptomatic hyponatremia (seizures): hypertonic (3%) saline in small boluses, closely monitored
- SIADH: fluid restriction first-line; vasopressin receptor antagonists (tolvaptan) or other agents in refractory cases

*High-yield: The rate of sodium correction, not the absolute sodium value, is what determines risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome — this is one of the most tested points in electrolyte physiology.*

### Hypernatremia
- Definition: serum Na+ >145 mEq/L
- Usually reflects free water deficit relative to sodium (pure water loss, e.g., diabetes insipidus, insensible losses, inadequate intake — especially in elderly/infants with impaired thirst/access)
- Clinical: thirst, lethargy, confusion, seizures if severe
- Management: correct free water deficit gradually (similarly, avoid rapid correction — risk of cerebral edema if corrected too fast); address underlying cause (e.g., desmopressin for central DI)

## Potassium Disorders

### Hyperkalemia
- Causes: renal failure (impaired excretion), medications (ACEI/ARB, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs), acidosis (transcellular shift), tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis syndrome, hemolysis), Addison's disease
- ECG changes (progressive with severity): peaked T waves → PR prolongation, flattened P waves → widened QRS → sine wave pattern → asystole/VF
- Management (severity-based):
1. IV calcium gluconate — stabilizes cardiac membrane (fastest, doesn't lower K+, just protects heart) — give first if ECG changes present
2. Insulin + glucose — shifts K+ intracellularly (onset within 15-30 min)
3. Beta-2 agonists (nebulized salbutamol) — also shifts K+ intracellularly
4. Sodium bicarbonate — if concurrent acidosis
5. Removal of potassium: loop diuretics, potassium binders (patiromer/sodium zirconium cyclosilicate), dialysis (definitive, especially in renal failure/refractory cases)

*High-yield: Calcium gluconate does not lower serum potassium — it stabilizes the cardiac membrane to buy time while other measures shift/remove potassium.*

### Hypokalemia
- Causes: GI losses (vomiting, diarrhea), diuretics (loop, thiazide), hyperaldosteronism, renal tubular acidosis (type 1 and 2), insulin excess, beta-agonists, alkalosis (transcellular shift)
- ECG changes: flattened/inverted T waves, U waves, ST depression; risk of arrhythmia (especially with digoxin use — increased digoxin toxicity risk)
- Management: oral or IV potassium replacement (IV for severe/symptomatic — rate-limited to avoid iatrogenic hyperkalemia/cardiac effects); correct concurrent hypomagnesemia (refractory hypokalemia often has coexisting hypomagnesemia)

## Calcium Disorders

### Hypercalcemia
- Causes: primary hyperparathyroidism (most common cause in outpatients), malignancy (most common cause in hospitalized patients — via PTHrP, bony metastases, or lytic lesions in myeloma)
- Clinical mnemonic: "Stones, Bones, Groans, Thrones, Psychiatric overtones" (renal stones, bone pain, abdominal pain, polyuria, neuropsychiatric symptoms)
- ECG: shortened QT interval
- Management: IV fluids (normal saline) first-line, bisphosphonates (for malignancy-associated, slower onset), calcitonin (rapid but short-lived effect), treat underlying cause; dialysis in severe refractory cases

### Hypocalcemia
- Causes: hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy classic), vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, hypomagnesemia (impairs PTH secretion/action), acute pancreatitis (saponification)
- Clinical: tetany, Chvostek's sign (facial twitch on tapping facial nerve), Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation), perioral numbness, seizures
- ECG: prolonged QT interval (risk of torsades)
- Management: IV calcium gluconate for symptomatic/severe, oral calcium + vitamin D for chronic replacement; correct magnesium if low

## Acid-Base Disorders

### Approach
1. Look at pH: acidemia (<7.35) or alkalemia (>7.45)
2. Determine primary disorder: check PaCO2 (respiratory) and HCO3- (metabolic)
3. Assess compensation (expected direction and degree)
4. If metabolic acidosis: calculate anion gap = Na+ - (Cl- + HCO3-); normal ~8-12

### Metabolic Acidosis
| Anion gap | Causes |
|---|---|
| High anion gap | **MUDPILES**: Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Propylene glycol/Paraldehyde, Iron/Isoniazid, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates |
| Normal anion gap (hyperchloremic) | Diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis, early renal failure, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors |

- Winter's formula: expected PaCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3- + 8 (± 2) — checks for appropriate respiratory compensation in metabolic acidosis

### Metabolic Alkalosis
- Causes: vomiting/NG suction (loss of HCl), diuretics (loop/thiazide), hyperaldosteronism, severe hypokalemia
- Classify as chloride-responsive (volume-depleted, corrects with saline — most common, e.g., vomiting) vs chloride-resistant (e.g., hyperaldosteronism, Cushing's — doesn't correct with saline alone)

### Respiratory Acidosis
- Causes: hypoventilation — COPD, opioid overdose, neuromuscular disease, severe obesity (OHS)
- Acute vs chronic distinguished by degree of renal (metabolic) compensation (minimal in acute, significant HCO3- rise in chronic)

### Respiratory Alkalosis
- Causes: hyperventilation — anxiety, pain, high altitude, pulmonary embolism (classic early ABG finding), salicylate toxicity (early stage, before metabolic acidosis develops), sepsis, pregnancy (normal physiological mild respiratory alkalosis)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Correcting hyponatremia too rapidly risks osmotic demyelination syndrome — limit correction to ~8-10 mEq/L in 24 hours in most cases
- SIADH is the most common cause of euvolemic hyponatremia
- IV calcium gluconate stabilizes the cardiac membrane in hyperkalemia but does not lower serum potassium itself
- Peaked T waves are the earliest ECG sign of hyperkalemia; progression leads to a sine wave pattern and asystole
- Primary hyperparathyroidism is the most common cause of hypercalcemia in outpatients; malignancy is most common in hospitalized patients
- Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs are classic bedside findings of hypocalcemia
- MUDPILES mnemonic covers causes of high anion gap metabolic acidosis
- Salicylate toxicity classically causes an early respiratory alkalosis followed by a mixed picture with metabolic acidosis
- Hypomagnesemia should always be corrected in refractory hypokalemia, as magnesium deficiency impairs potassium repletion
