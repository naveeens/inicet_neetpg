# ARDS Respiratory Failure

## Respiratory Failure — Classification
| Type | Mechanism | ABG pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Type 1 (hypoxemic) | V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion defect | ↓PaO2, normal/↓PaCO2 |
| Type 2 (hypercapnic) | Alveolar hypoventilation (pump failure — neuromuscular disease, CNS depression, severe airway obstruction) | ↑PaCO2, ↓PaO2 |

### Causes
- Type 1: pneumonia, ARDS, pulmonary edema, PE, pneumothorax
- Type 2: COPD exacerbation, opioid overdose/CNS depression, neuromuscular disease (myasthenia, Guillain-Barré), severe asthma, chest wall/respiratory muscle disease

## Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

### Definition — Berlin Criteria
1. Acute onset (within 1 week of a known clinical insult)
2. Bilateral opacities on chest imaging not fully explained by effusion/collapse/nodules
3. Respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure/fluid overload (need objective assessment, e.g., echo, to exclude cardiogenic edema if no clear risk factor)
4. Hypoxemia severity (PaO2/FiO2 ratio on PEEP ≥5 cmH2O):

| Severity | PaO2/FiO2 |
|---|---|
| Mild | 200–300 |
| Moderate | 100–200 |
| Severe | <100 |

### Etiology
- Direct lung injury: pneumonia, aspiration, pulmonary contusion, inhalational injury
- Indirect (systemic): sepsis (most common cause overall), severe trauma, pancreatitis, massive transfusion, drug overdose

### Pathophysiology
Diffuse alveolar damage → increased capillary permeability → protein-rich pulmonary edema (non-cardiogenic) → impaired gas exchange, decreased lung compliance. Exudative phase (diffuse alveolar damage, hyaline membranes) → proliferative phase → fibrotic phase (in some patients).

### Management
- Treat underlying cause (e.g., source control/antibiotics for sepsis)
- Lung-protective mechanical ventilation: low tidal volume (~6 mL/kg predicted body weight), plateau pressure <30 cmH2O — reduces ventilator-induced lung injury (landmark trial evidence, foundational to modern ARDS management)
- Adequate PEEP to improve oxygenation/recruit alveoli while avoiding overdistension
- Prone positioning: improves oxygenation and mortality in moderate-severe ARDS (landmark trial evidence) — used for a sustained duration (typically ~16 hours/day) in severe hypoxemia
- Conservative fluid management (once shock resolved) — reduces days on ventilator
- Neuromuscular blockade / deep sedation in select severe cases with ventilator dyssynchrony
- ECMO: rescue therapy in refractory severe ARDS at specialized centers
- Avoid routine high-dose corticosteroids historically, though role has been revisited in specific ARDS subgroups/etiologies (e.g., COVID-19 ARDS) — evolving evidence, verify against latest guideline

*High-yield: Low tidal volume ("lung-protective") ventilation is the single most evidence-based intervention proven to reduce mortality in ARDS.*

## Acute Respiratory Failure — General Management Principles
- Secure airway, assess need for intubation/mechanical ventilation (based on work of breathing, mental status, ABG trends — not ABG numbers alone)
- Oxygen therapy titrated to target saturation (caution in chronic Type 2 respiratory failure/COPD — risk of worsening hypercapnia with uncontrolled high-flow oxygen)
- Non-invasive ventilation (NIV/BiPAP): effective in COPD exacerbation, cardiogenic pulmonary edema; use cautiously/avoid in ARDS with severe hypoxemia (higher failure rates, risk of delayed intubation) — evidence and practice on NIV in ARDS is evolving, verify against latest guideline
- Address and treat the underlying precipitant

## High-Yield One-Liners
- ARDS Berlin criteria: acute onset (<1 week), bilateral opacities, not fully explained by cardiac failure, PaO2/FiO2 <300
- Sepsis is the most common cause/association of ARDS
- Low tidal volume ventilation (~6 mL/kg predicted body weight) is the cornerstone, mortality-reducing intervention in ARDS
- Prone positioning improves oxygenation and survival in moderate-severe ARDS
- ARDS causes non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema — distinguishing it from cardiogenic edema (heart failure) is a key diagnostic step (Berlin criteria requirement)
- Type 1 respiratory failure = hypoxemia without significant hypercapnia; Type 2 = hypoxemia with hypercapnia (ventilatory pump failure)
- Uncontrolled high-flow oxygen in chronic Type 2 respiratory failure (e.g., COPD) can worsen hypercapnia — titrate carefully
