# Infective Endocarditis Rheumatic Heart Disease

## Infective Endocarditis (IE)

### Definition/Pathophysiology
Infection of endocardial surface (usually valves) — endothelial damage/turbulent flow → platelet-fibrin thrombus (nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis) → bacteremia seeds thrombus → vegetation.

### Etiology
| Setting | Common organism |
|---|---|
| Native valve (community) | Streptococcus viridans (subacute, post-dental procedures) |
| Native valve, acute/aggressive | Staphylococcus aureus (most common cause of IE overall now, especially acute presentation) |
| IV drug users | S. aureus (classically tricuspid valve) |
| Prosthetic valve (early, <60 days) | Coagulase-negative staphylococci (S. epidermidis) |
| Prosthetic valve (late) | Similar to native valve organisms |
| Colon cancer association | Streptococcus bovis (S. gallolyticus) |
| Culture-negative / HACEK group | Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella |
| Nosocomial/catheter-associated | Staphylococci |

### Clinical Features
- Fever (most common symptom), new/changing murmur
- Immunologic phenomena: Osler nodes (tender), Roth spots (retinal hemorrhage with pale center), glomerulonephritis
- Embolic phenomena: Janeway lesions (painless, palms/soles), splinter hemorrhages, splenic/renal/cerebral emboli
- Splenomegaly, clubbing (subacute/chronic cases)

### Diagnosis — Modified Duke Criteria
| Major criteria | Minor criteria |
|---|---|
| Positive blood culture (typical organism, 2 separate cultures) | Predisposing heart condition or IV drug use |
| Evidence of endocardial involvement (vegetation on echo, new regurgitant murmur) | Fever ≥38°C |
| | Vascular phenomena (emboli, Janeway lesions) |
| | Immunologic phenomena (Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis, rheumatoid factor) |
| | Positive blood culture not meeting major criteria |

- Definite IE: 2 major, OR 1 major + 3 minor, OR 5 minor
- Echocardiography: TTE first-line; TEE more sensitive (especially prosthetic valves, small vegetations)
- Blood cultures: 3 sets from different sites before starting antibiotics

### Management
- Prolonged IV antibiotics (typically 4–6 weeks) based on organism/sensitivity — empirical then organism-directed
- Surgery indications: heart failure refractory to medical therapy, uncontrolled infection/abscess, recurrent emboli despite antibiotics, fungal endocarditis, prosthetic valve dehiscence
- Antibiotic prophylaxis (current guidelines much more restrictive than historically): only for high-risk cardiac lesions (prosthetic valve, prior IE, certain congenital heart disease, cardiac transplant with valvulopathy) undergoing high-risk dental procedures — amoxicillin single dose before procedure

*High-yield: Prophylaxis indications have narrowed considerably over guideline revisions — older exam answers requiring prophylaxis for "MVP with regurgitation" or rheumatic valve disease alone are now outdated; current guidance restricts to the highest-risk group listed above.*

### Complications
- Heart failure, valve destruction/perforation, embolic stroke, mycotic aneurysm, glomerulonephritis (immune complex-mediated), abscess (perivalvular/splenic)

## Rheumatic Heart Disease

### Pathophysiology
Post-streptococcal (Group A Streptococcus pharyngitis) sequela — molecular mimicry (antibodies against M protein cross-react with cardiac myosin/valve tissue) → autoimmune valvulitis. Acute rheumatic fever precedes chronic RHD after latent period (weeks).

### Diagnosis — Jones Criteria (Acute Rheumatic Fever)
| Major | Minor |
|---|---|
| Carditis | Arthralgia (fever if carditis is major) |
| Migratory polyarthritis (large joints) | Fever |
| Chorea (Sydenham's) | Elevated ESR/CRP |
| Erythema marginatum | Prolonged PR interval on ECG |
| Subcutaneous nodules | |

- Diagnosis: 2 major, OR 1 major + 2 minor + evidence of preceding GAS infection (ASO titer, throat culture, rapid antigen test, recent scarlet fever)

### Chronic RHD — Valve Involvement
- Mitral valve most commonly affected (mitral stenosis is the classic chronic sequela); mitral regurgitation common in acute carditis
- Aortic valve second most common
- "Fish mouth" mitral stenosis, later calcification

### Management
- Acute rheumatic fever: high-dose aspirin/NSAIDs for arthritis/carditis, penicillin to eradicate GAS
- Secondary prophylaxis: benzathine penicillin G IM every 3–4 weeks (duration depends on carditis severity — 5 years/until age 21 for fever alone up to lifelong for severe carditis/valve disease — per current knowledge, verify against latest guideline)
- Chronic RHD: manage heart failure/arrhythmia (esp. AF from mitral disease), anticoagulation if AF/mechanical valve, surgical valve repair/replacement when indicated

## Mnemonic (IE — "FROM JANE")
**F**ever, **R**oth spots, **O**sler nodes, **M**urmur, **J**aneway lesions, **A**nemia, **N**ail hemorrhage (splinter), **E**mboli

## High-Yield One-Liners
- S. aureus = most common overall cause of infective endocarditis (especially acute, IV drug users — tricuspid valve)
- Streptococcus viridans = classic subacute IE following dental procedures
- Strep bovis bacteremia/endocarditis → mandates colonoscopy to rule out colon malignancy
- Osler nodes are tender (painful), Janeway lesions are painless — classic exam differentiator
- Mitral valve is most commonly affected in chronic rheumatic heart disease; mitral stenosis is the hallmark late lesion
- Secondary penicillin prophylaxis is the mainstay to prevent recurrent rheumatic fever and progressive valve damage
- Sydenham's chorea may appear months after the initial GAS infection, often as an isolated major criterion
