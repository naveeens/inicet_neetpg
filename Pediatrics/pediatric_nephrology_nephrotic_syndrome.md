# Pediatric Nephrology Nephrotic Syndrome

## Nephrotic Syndrome — Definition
Clinical syndrome characterized by:
- **Massive proteinuria** (urine protein-creatinine ratio >2, or 24-hr urine protein >40 mg/m²/hr, or 3-4+ on dipstick)
- **Hypoalbuminemia** (serum albumin <2.5 g/dL)
- **Edema** (periorbital initially, then generalized/anasarca — dependent, pitting)
- **Hyperlipidemia** (compensatory hepatic lipoprotein synthesis in response to low oncotic pressure)

## Etiology / Classification

### Idiopathic (Primary) — Most Common in Children
| Type | % of Childhood NS | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimal Change Disease (MCD)** | ~80-90% | Most common cause overall in children (peak age 2-6 years); normal glomeruli on light microscopy, **effacement of podocyte foot processes** on EM; excellent steroid response |
| Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) | ~7-10% | More steroid-resistant, worse prognosis, higher risk of progression to CKD |
| Membranous Nephropathy | Rare in children | More common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults |
| Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN) | Uncommon | Associated with low C3, can have nephritic features too |

### Secondary Causes
- Post-infectious (Hepatitis B, HIV, malaria)
- Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP) nephritis
- SLE nephritis
- Drugs (NSAIDs, gold)
- Congenital nephrotic syndrome (Finnish type — autosomal recessive, NPHS1/nephrin gene mutation, presents in first 3 months of life)

*High-yield: In a child 2-6 years with nephrotic syndrome and NO hematuria/hypertension/renal impairment, presume Minimal Change Disease and start empirical steroids WITHOUT biopsy — biopsy reserved for atypical features or steroid resistance/dependence.*

## Clinical Features
- Periorbital edema (often noticed first, may be mistaken for allergy), progressing to generalized edema/ascites/pleural effusion/scrotal edema
- Frothy urine (due to proteinuria)
- Weight gain (fluid retention)
- Normal blood pressure and normal renal function typical in MCD (atypical features suggest FSGS/other causes)
- Increased susceptibility to infections (loss of immunoglobulins in urine, immunosuppressive therapy) — especially **Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis** classically from Streptococcus pneumoniae, and cellulitis
- Increased risk of **thromboembolism** (loss of antithrombin III in urine, hemoconcentration, hyperfibrinogenemia) — renal vein thrombosis classic complication

## Investigations
- Urine routine: proteinuria (3-4+), may show hyaline/fatty casts; microscopic hematuria uncommon in MCD (if present, think FSGS or other glomerulopathy)
- Urine protein: creatinine ratio (spot) — convenient quantification
- Serum albumin (low), total protein (low), lipid profile (elevated cholesterol/triglycerides)
- Serum creatinine/urea — usually normal in MCD
- Complement levels (C3): normal in MCD/FSGS; low in post-infectious GN, SLE, MPGN
- Renal biopsy — NOT routine for typical presentation; indicated for:
  - Age <1 year or >12 years at onset
  - Steroid resistance
  - Atypical features: gross hematuria, hypertension, renal failure, persistent low complement
  - Frequent relapsers before starting second-line immunosuppressants (in some protocols)

## Management

### Steroid Therapy (First-Line for Presumed MCD)
- **Prednisolone** at standard dose (per current protocols, typically ~2 mg/kg/day or 60 mg/m²/day, max ~60 mg/day) for an initial period (commonly 4-6 weeks), followed by tapering/alternate-day therapy over additional weeks — exact duration varies by protocol (ISKDC vs newer guidelines), verify current national/institutional protocol
- **Response definition**: urine protein-free (or albumin-trace) for 3 consecutive days = complete remission
- Most children with MCD (~90%) respond to steroids — "steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (SSNS)"

### Terminology (High Yield — Frequently Tested)
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Remission** | Urine protein nil/trace for 3 consecutive days |
| **Relapse** | Proteinuria 3-4+ for 3 consecutive days after remission |
| **Frequent relapser** | ≥2 relapses in 6 months after initial response, or ≥4 relapses in any 12-month period |
| **Steroid dependent** | Relapse during steroid taper or within 2 weeks of stopping steroids, on 2 consecutive occasions |
| **Steroid resistant** | Failure to achieve remission despite adequate steroid therapy for 4-8 weeks (varies per protocol) |

### Management of Relapses / Steroid Resistance
- Relapses: retreat with steroids (often shorter course)
- Frequent relapsers/steroid-dependent: steroid-sparing agents — **Levamisole**, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine/tacrolimus), rituximab (in refractory cases)
- Steroid-resistant: strongly consider renal biopsy (likely FSGS or other pathology), calcineurin inhibitors often used

### Supportive Management
- Salt restriction during edema phase
- Diuretics (used cautiously — risk of intravascular volume depletion/thrombosis if overused, since these children may already be intravascularly depleted despite total body fluid overload)
- Albumin infusion — only for severe symptomatic hypoalbuminemia/edema with compromise (not routine)
- Vaccination: **avoid live vaccines while on high-dose steroids/immunosuppression**; pneumococcal vaccination recommended (increased risk of pneumococcal peritonitis/sepsis); annual influenza vaccine
- Monitor for and treat infections promptly (high suspicion for peritonitis/cellulitis)
- Prophylactic anticoagulation considered in high-risk situations (severe hypoalbuminemia, immobility)

## Complications
- Infections (spontaneous bacterial peritonitis — pneumococcus classically, cellulitis, sepsis)
- Thromboembolism (renal vein thrombosis, other venous thrombosis)
- Acute kidney injury (hypovolemia, or intrinsic in some cases)
- Growth failure, cushingoid features, cataracts, hypertension, osteoporosis — from chronic/repeated steroid use
- Progression to CKD — more likely in FSGS/steroid-resistant disease than in classic MCD

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Minimal Change Disease is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children (2-6 years peak)
- EM in MCD shows effacement of podocyte foot processes; light microscopy is normal
- Typical presentation in a young child with no hematuria/hypertension → treat empirically with steroids, biopsy not needed initially
- Complement (C3) is normal in MCD and FSGS, but low in post-infectious GN, SLE nephritis, and MPGN
- Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in nephrotic syndrome is classically caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae
- Renal vein thrombosis is a feared thromboembolic complication due to loss of antithrombin III and hemoconcentration
- Avoid live vaccines during high-dose steroid/immunosuppressive therapy
- Steroid resistance or atypical features (hematuria, hypertension, renal impairment, age extremes) are indications for renal biopsy
