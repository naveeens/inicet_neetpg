# Neonatology Birth Asphyxia Jaundice RDS

## Birth Asphyxia / Perinatal Asphyxia

### Definition
Failure to initiate/sustain spontaneous breathing at birth, leading to hypoxia, hypercapnia, and acidosis. Diagnosed clinically + supported by cord blood gas (pH <7.0) and low Apgar scores.

### APGAR Score (assessed at 1 and 5 minutes; repeat at 10 min if <7 at 5 min)
| Sign | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A**ppearance (color) | Blue/pale | Body pink, extremities blue | All pink |
| **P**ulse (HR) | Absent | <100/min | >100/min |
| **G**rimace (reflex irritability) | No response | Grimace | Cry/withdrawal |
| **A**ctivity (tone) | Limp | Some flexion | Active motion |
| **R**espiration | Absent | Slow/irregular | Good/crying |

- Score 7-10: normal; 4-6: moderate depression; 0-3: severe depression
- 1-min score correlates with need for resuscitation; **5-min score correlates better with neurological outcome/prognosis**
- Apgar score is NOT used to decide whether to initiate resuscitation (resuscitation decisions are based on breathing, tone, HR — start immediately, don't wait for score)

### Neonatal Resuscitation (NRP algorithm — high yield)
- Golden minute: dry, stimulate, position, assess breathing & HR within first 60 seconds
- If not breathing/gasping or HR <100: **Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV)** with room air (term) initially
- Most important/effective step in neonatal resuscitation = **adequate ventilation** (not chest compressions, not drugs)
- Chest compressions started if HR <60/min despite 30 sec of effective PPV; ratio 3:1 (compressions:ventilation)
- Epinephrine (IV/UVC preferred over ET) if HR remains <60 despite compressions + ventilation
- Target SpO2 rises gradually; use pulse oximetry to titrate oxygen, avoid hyperoxia

### Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE) — Sarnat Staging
| Stage | Consciousness | Tone | Reflexes | Seizures |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I (Mild) | Hyperalert, irritable | Normal/mild hypertonia | Exaggerated | None |
| II (Moderate) | Lethargic | Hypotonia | Weak/absent | Common |
| III (Severe) | Stupor/coma | Flaccid | Absent | Rare (suppressed EEG) |

- **Therapeutic hypothermia** (33.5°C for 72 hours, initiated within 6 hours of birth) — standard of care for moderate-severe HIE, reduces death/disability
- Stage I usually complete recovery; Stage III has highest mortality/severe disability

## Neonatal Jaundice / Hyperbilirubinemia

### Physiological vs Pathological Jaundice
| Feature | Physiological | Pathological |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | After 24 hours of life | Within 24 hours |
| Peak | Day 3-4 (term), day 5-7 (preterm) | Any time, rapid rise |
| Rate of rise | <5 mg/dL/day (<0.2 mg/dL/hr) | >5 mg/dL/day |
| Peak level | <15 mg/dL (term) | Higher, or any level with risk factors |
| Duration | Resolves by 1 week (term)/2 weeks (preterm) | Persists beyond |
| Conjugated fraction | <20% of total | Often elevated, or other causes |

*High-yield: Jaundice appearing in first 24 hours is ALWAYS pathological — think hemolysis (Rh/ABO incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, sepsis).*

### Causes of Pathological Jaundice
- **Hemolytic**: Rh incompatibility, ABO incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, spherocytosis
- **Increased enterohepatic circulation**: breast milk jaundice, breastfeeding failure jaundice, intestinal obstruction
- **Decreased conjugation**: Crigler-Najjar, Gilbert syndrome, hypothyroidism
- **Cholestatic (conjugated)**: biliary atresia, neonatal hepatitis, TORCH infections, sepsis, TPN-associated

### Breastfeeding Jaundice vs Breast Milk Jaundice (Classic Exam Pair)
| | Breastfeeding (failure) Jaundice | Breast Milk Jaundice |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | First week (2-4 days) | After first week, peaks 2nd week |
| Cause | Inadequate intake → ↑ enterohepatic circulation | Substance in breast milk inhibiting glucuronyl transferase (β-glucuronidase) |
| Management | Increase feeding frequency, lactation support | Usually benign, continue breastfeeding, monitor; rarely need brief interruption |

### Kernicterus
- Bilirubin-induced neurologic damage — deposition in basal ganglia, hippocampus, cranial nerve nuclei
- Risk increases with unconjugated bilirubin, especially with prematurity, sepsis, acidosis, hypoalbuminemia (factors that increase free/unbound bilirubin or disrupt blood-brain barrier)
- Acute: lethargy, poor feeding → hypertonia, opisthotonus, high-pitched cry, seizures
- Chronic sequelae: choreoathetoid cerebral palsy, sensorineural hearing loss, upward gaze palsy, dental enamel dysplasia

### Management
- **Phototherapy**: blue light (~460-490 nm) converts unconjugated bilirubin to water-soluble photoisomers; threshold based on age-in-hours + risk-factor-specific nomograms (Bhutani/AAP charts)
- **Exchange transfusion**: for very high bilirubin levels or signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy, or rapid rise despite intensive phototherapy — removes bilirubin and antibody-coated cells (double volume exchange)
- Adequate hydration/feeding always; IVIG may be used as adjunct in isoimmune hemolytic disease to reduce need for exchange transfusion

## Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) / Hyaline Membrane Disease

### Etiology
- **Surfactant deficiency** in preterm infants (surfactant production adequate only after ~34-35 weeks gestation)
- Surfactant (produced by Type II pneumocytes) reduces alveolar surface tension, prevents atelectasis
- Risk factors: prematurity (single biggest risk factor), maternal diabetes, cesarean section without labor, male sex, second twin, perinatal asphyxia
- Antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone/dexamethasone) to mother in preterm labor accelerate fetal lung maturity and reduce RDS incidence/severity

### Clinical Features
- Onset within minutes to hours of birth in a preterm infant
- Tachypnea, grunting, nasal flaring, intercostal/subcostal retractions, cyanosis
- Progressive worsening over first 24-48 hours if untreated, then improvement (unlike TTN which resolves faster, or pneumonia which may worsen with sepsis signs)

### Diagnosis
- Chest X-ray: **diffuse ground-glass/reticulogranular pattern with air bronchograms**, low lung volumes, "white-out" in severe cases
- Lecithin:Sphingomyelin (L:S) ratio in amniotic fluid — **>2 = fetal lung maturity** (mature), <1.5 = high risk of RDS (antenatal predictive test)

### Management
- Antenatal: corticosteroids to mother (if preterm labor 24-34 weeks) — single most effective preventive measure
- Postnatal: **Exogenous surfactant therapy** (via ET tube or minimally invasive surfactant therapy) — mainstay of treatment
- Respiratory support: CPAP (early use reduces need for mechanical ventilation), mechanical ventilation if severe
- Supportive: thermoregulation, adequate oxygenation (avoid hyperoxia — risk of retinopathy of prematurity), careful fluid management

### Complications
- Acute: pneumothorax (air leak), intraventricular hemorrhage, patent ductus arteriosus
- Chronic: bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) — especially with prolonged mechanical ventilation/oxygen therapy
- Retinopathy of prematurity — linked to uncontrolled oxygen therapy

## High-Yield One-Liners
- Apgar at 5 minutes correlates better with long-term neurological outcome than 1-minute score; resuscitation is never delayed to calculate Apgar
- Most important step in neonatal resuscitation is effective ventilation, not chest compressions or epinephrine
- Therapeutic hypothermia (started within 6 hours) is standard of care for moderate-severe HIE
- Jaundice within first 24 hours of life is always pathological — suspect hemolysis
- Kernicterus is bilirubin deposition in basal ganglia — classic sequela is choreoathetoid cerebral palsy
- RDS = surfactant deficiency in preterm neonates; CXR shows ground-glass appearance with air bronchograms
- L:S ratio >2 in amniotic fluid indicates fetal lung maturity
- Antenatal corticosteroids are the single most effective measure to prevent/reduce severity of RDS in anticipated preterm delivery
- Exogenous surfactant + CPAP are mainstays of postnatal RDS management
