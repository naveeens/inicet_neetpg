# CNS Special Senses Physiology

## Synaptic Transmission Basics
- Chemical synapse: presynaptic AP → Ca2+ influx → neurotransmitter release → binds postsynaptic receptors → **EPSP** (excitatory, usually Na+ influx via ionotropic receptors) or **IPSP** (inhibitory, usually Cl- influx/K+ efflux)
- **Spatial summation**: multiple synapses active simultaneously; **Temporal summation**: repeated firing of same synapse in rapid succession
- Major neurotransmitters: Glutamate (main excitatory, NMDA/AMPA/kainate receptors), GABA (main inhibitory in brain, Cl- channel — GABA-A; GABA-B is Gi-coupled), Glycine (inhibitory, spinal cord), Dopamine, Norepinephrine, Serotonin, Acetylcholine

## Ascending Sensory Pathways
| Pathway | Modality | Decussation | Key relay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dorsal column-medial lemniscus (DCML) | Fine touch, proprioception, vibration, 2-point discrimination | Medulla (internal arcuate fibers → contralateral medial lemniscus) | Gracile (lower limb)/Cuneate (upper limb) nucleus → VPL thalamus → cortex |
| Anterolateral (spinothalamic) system | Pain, temperature, crude touch | **Spinal cord**, at/near level of entry (within 1-2 segments) | Anterior (crude touch) + Lateral (pain/temp) spinothalamic tracts → VPL thalamus |
| Dorsal spinocerebellar / Ventral spinocerebellar | Unconscious proprioception to cerebellum | Does not reach cortex | Cerebellum (ipsilateral mainly) |

*High-yield: DCML decussates in the medulla (high up); spinothalamic decussates in the spinal cord near the level of entry — this difference explains the classic dissociated sensory loss patterns in Brown-Sequard syndrome and syringomyelia.*

- **Brown-Sequard syndrome** (cord hemisection): ipsilateral loss of fine touch/proprioception + motor (corticospinal, not yet decussated) below lesion; contralateral loss of pain/temperature below lesion (already decussated at cord level)
- **Syringomyelia**: central cord damage → bilateral loss of pain/temperature (crossing spinothalamic fibers) with preserved fine touch — "cape-like" sensory loss

## Descending Motor Pathways
| Tract | Origin | Function | Decussation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lateral corticospinal | Motor cortex (mainly area 4) | Fine voluntary movement, distal limb muscles | Decussates in medullary pyramids |
| Anterior corticospinal | Motor cortex | Axial/proximal muscles | Decussates at level of termination (bilateral control) |
| Rubrospinal | Red nucleus | Flexor tone, upper limb | Decussates in midbrain |
| Vestibulospinal | Vestibular nuclei | Extensor tone, balance | Uncrossed |
| Reticulospinal | Reticular formation | Muscle tone, posture | Mixed |

- **Upper motor neuron (UMN) lesion**: spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign positive, no fasciculations, no muscle atrophy (early)
- **Lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion**: flaccidity, hyporeflexia/areflexia, Babinski absent, fasciculations, muscle atrophy

## Cerebellum
- **Vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular lobe)**: balance, eye movements — damage → truncal ataxia, nystagmus
- **Spinocerebellum (vermis + paravermis)**: posture, gait, muscle tone — damage → truncal/gait ataxia (vermis), limb ataxia (paravermis/intermediate zone)
- **Cerebrocerebellum (lateral hemispheres)**: planning/coordination of skilled movement — damage → intention tremor, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia
- Cerebellar signs are **ipsilateral** to the lesion (unlike cerebral lesions)

## Basal Ganglia
- Components: caudate, putamen (= striatum), globus pallidus, substantia nigra, subthalamic nucleus
- **Direct pathway** (Go): striatum → GPi (inhibits) → thalamus disinhibited → facilitates movement; dopamine (D1) facilitates this pathway
- **Indirect pathway** (No-go): striatum → GPe → STN → GPi → more thalamic inhibition → suppresses movement; dopamine (D2) inhibits this pathway
- **Parkinson's disease**: loss of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra pars compacta → net ↑ inhibition of thalamus → bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor (pill-rolling), postural instability
- **Huntington's disease**: loss of indirect pathway striatal neurons → net ↓ inhibition → chorea (excess movement)

## Hypothalamic Functions (Localization — Classic MCQ)
| Region | Function |
|---|---|
| Anterior/preoptic | Heat loss (cooling), parasympathetic, satiety (ventromedial specifically) |
| Posterior | Heat conservation/production, sympathetic |
| Ventromedial nucleus | Satiety center ("stop eating") — lesion → hyperphagia/obesity |
| Lateral | Hunger/feeding center ("start eating") — lesion → aphagia |
| Supraoptic/Paraventricular | ADH/Oxytocin synthesis |
| Suprachiasmatic nucleus | Circadian rhythm (master clock, entrained by light via retinohypothalamic tract) |

## Limbic System & Memory
- **Hippocampus**: consolidation of new declarative (explicit) memory into long-term storage — bilateral hippocampal damage (e.g., HM patient) → anterograde amnesia, procedural memory intact
- **Amygdala**: fear, emotional processing, autonomic response to emotion
- **Papez circuit**: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → mammillothalamic tract → anterior thalamus → cingulate gyrus → back to hippocampus
- Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome (thiamine deficiency, often alcoholics): mammillary body + thalamic damage → confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia (Wernicke's) + anterograde/retrograde amnesia with confabulation (Korsakoff's)

## Vision
- **Retina**: rods (night/scotopic vision, rhodopsin, peripheral, high sensitivity/low acuity, absent in fovea) vs cones (day/photopic vision, color, central/fovea, high acuity)
- Phototransduction: light → rhodopsin (opsin + retinal) → 11-cis to all-trans retinal → activates transducin (Gt) → activates phosphodiesterase → ↓ cGMP → closes Na+ channels → **hyperpolarization** (unique — photoreceptors hyperpolarize in response to light, unlike most sensory receptors)
- **Visual pathway**: retina → optic nerve → optic chiasm (nasal fibers decussate) → optic tract → **LGN of thalamus** → optic radiation → primary visual cortex (occipital, area 17/V1)
- **Lesion localization** (classic exam topic):
  - Optic nerve: monocular blindness
  - Optic chiasm (e.g., pituitary tumor): **bitemporal hemianopia** (compresses crossing nasal fibers)
  - Optic tract: contralateral homonymous hemianopia
  - Optic radiation (temporal — Meyer's loop): contralateral homonymous **superior** quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky")
  - Optic radiation (parietal): contralateral homonymous **inferior** quadrantanopia
  - Occipital cortex (V1): contralateral homonymous hemianopia, macular sparing (dual blood supply — PCA + MCA collateral)
- **Accommodation reflex**: near object → lens rounds up (ciliary muscle contraction, relaxes zonular fibers, mediated by parasympathetic/CN III), pupil constricts (miosis), convergence
- **Pupillary light reflex**: afferent CN II → pretectal nucleus → Edinger-Westphal nucleus (bilateral) → efferent CN III (parasympathetic) → pupillary constriction — consensual reflex due to bilateral E-W projection

## Hearing
- Sound: external ear → tympanic membrane → ossicles (malleus-incus-stapes, impedance matching, amplify pressure) → oval window → cochlea (perilymph/endolymph) → **organ of Corti (hair cells) on basilar membrane** → mechanotransduction (stereocilia bending opens K+ channels — endolymph rich in K+) → CN VIII (cochlear nerve) → cochlear nuclei → superior olivary complex → lateral lemniscus → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body (thalamus) → auditory cortex (temporal, area 41)
- **Tonotopic organization**: base of cochlea (near oval window, stiffer/narrower basilar membrane) = high frequency; apex (wider/floppier) = low frequency
- **Conductive vs sensorineural hearing loss**:
  - Rinne test: air conduction > bone conduction normally; conductive loss → bone conduction > air conduction (BC>AC) on affected side
  - Weber test: conductive loss → lateralizes to affected (worse) ear; sensorineural loss → lateralizes to normal (better) ear

## Taste & Smell
- Taste: 5 basic qualities — sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami; taste buds on tongue papillae; CN VII (anterior 2/3 tongue), CN IX (posterior 1/3), CN X (epiglottis/pharynx) → nucleus tractus solitarius → thalamus → gustatory cortex
- Smell: olfactory receptor neurons (bipolar, in olfactory epithelium) → olfactory nerve (CN I) → olfactory bulb → olfactory tract → **directly to olfactory/piriform cortex and amygdala** — unique among senses in bypassing the thalamus for primary processing

## Thermoregulation
- Hypothalamus = master thermostat (anterior = heat loss, posterior = heat conservation)
- Fever: pyrogens (IL-1, IL-6, TNF) → ↑ PGE2 in hypothalamus → raises set point → shivering, vasoconstriction to reach new (higher) set point; antipyretics (aspirin/paracetamol) inhibit PGE2 synthesis (COX inhibition)

## High-Yield One-Liners
- DCML decussates in the medulla; spinothalamic tract decussates at the spinal cord level — the basis for Brown-Sequard syndrome's dissociated sensory pattern
- UMN lesion: spasticity + hyperreflexia + Babinski positive, no atrophy; LMN lesion: flaccidity + hyporeflexia + fasciculations + atrophy
- Cerebellar lesions produce ipsilateral signs; cerebral lesions produce contralateral signs
- Parkinsonism = dopamine deficiency in substantia nigra → net increased basal ganglia inhibitory output → bradykinesia/rigidity/tremor; Huntington's = striatal degeneration → chorea
- Ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus = satiety center (lesion → obesity); lateral hypothalamic nucleus = hunger center (lesion → aphagia)
- Photoreceptors are unique in hyperpolarizing (not depolarizing) in response to their stimulus (light)
- Optic chiasm lesion (classically pituitary adenoma) → bitemporal hemianopia; Meyer's loop (temporal radiation) lesion → contralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky")
- Occipital cortex lesions typically spare macular vision due to dual (PCA + MCA) blood supply
- Rinne + Weber tests distinguish conductive (BC>AC, lateralizes to bad ear) from sensorineural (AC>BC, lateralizes to good ear) hearing loss
- Olfactory pathway is the only sensory pathway that bypasses the thalamus en route to primary cortex
- Hippocampus is essential for consolidating new declarative memory; bilateral damage causes anterograde amnesia with preserved procedural learning
